# Restatement

## Abstract

The talks at the meeting covered several aspects of noncommutative geometry. The topics included:

- new results on the geometry of the noncommutative torus;
- local index formulae in various settings;
- C\*-algebras and dynamical systems associated with number-theoretic structures;
- new methods in K-theory for noncommutative algebras;
- new progress in quantum field theory using concepts from noncommutative geometry.

## 1. Introduction, two key examples

### Spaces that are noncommutative in the classical sense

The following spaces are given as examples of spaces that are "noncommutative" in the sense that classical tools do not apply:

- the space of leaves of a foliation;
- the space of irreducible representations of a discrete group;
- the space of Penrose tilings;
- the space of infinite geodesics in a negatively curved compact Riemann surface;
- the space of rank one subgroups of ℝ².

For such spaces, the cardinality equals that of the continuum, but they cannot be put constructively in bijection with the continuum. Any explicitly constructed map from such a set to the real line is not injective.

The basic principle is to take a space presented as a quotient of an ordinary space by an equivalence relation, and to associate to the equivalence relation its convolution algebra over ℂ. The groupoid identifying two points gives the algebra M₂(ℂ). When the quotient exists as an ordinary space, the algebra is Morita equivalent to the commutative algebra of complex-valued functions on the quotient.

### Space-time and the Standard Model

The group Diff(M) of diffeomorphisms of M is the group of automorphisms of the star algebra 𝒜 of smooth functions on M. Replacing 𝒜 by the Morita equivalent algebra M_n(𝒜) enlarges the group of automorphisms in the way required by a non-abelian gauge theory with gauge group SU(n).

A geometric space is encoded by its algebra of coordinates 𝒜 and its "line element," which specifies the metric. The spectral-triple paradigm (see § 2) encodes the discrete and the continuum on one Hilbert space.

The distance between points a, b and between states φ, ψ is given by

$$d(a,b)=\sup|f(a)-f(b)|,\quad f \text{ such that } \|[D,f]\|\le 1, \tag{2}$$

$$d(\phi,\psi)=\sup|\phi(f)-\psi(f)|,\quad f \text{ such that } \|[D,f]\|\le 1. \tag{3}$$

The Yukawa coupling matrix D_F of the Standard Model gives the inverse line element for the finite geometry (𝒜_F, ℋ_F, D_F).

The finite algebra is

$$\mathcal{A}_F=C_+\oplus C_-,\qquad C_+=M_2(\mathbb{H}),\qquad C_-=M_4(\mathbb{C}). \tag{18}$$

This algebra arises both from a bottom-up adaptation of the finite geometry to particles and forces and from a purely geometric problem. The simultaneous quantization of the fundamental class in K-homology and in K-theory [32, 31] is cited as explaining the finite algebra. In this approach, the K-homology fundamental class is represented by the Dirac operator. The K-theory fundamental class is represented using the Feynman slash of the coordinates, which accounts for the non-commutativity of 𝒜_F arising from Clifford algebras.

The full algebra of coordinates is generated by adjoining a unitary Y of K-theoretic nature with Y⁴ = 1. The noncommutativity of the resulting system generates the continuum and allows one to write a higher analogue of the commutation relations that quantizes the volume and encodes all 4-dimensional spin geometries.

### Zeta function and adèle class space

The operation on functions f(u) of a positive real variable u is

$$f\mapsto E(f),\qquad E(f)(v):=\sum_{\mathbb{N}^\times} f(nv). \tag{1}$$

In the variable log v, this operation is a sum of translations log v ↦ log v + log n. After a suitable Fourier transform it becomes multiplication by the Fourier transform of the sum of Dirac masses δ_{log n}, namely the Riemann zeta function Σ e^{−is log n} = ζ(is). A spectral realization is therefore obtained by considering the cokernel of E.

The space of adèle classes is

$$X=\mathbb{Q}^*\backslash\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q}/\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^*=\mathbb{Q}^*\backslash(Y\times\mathbb{R}),$$

where Y = 𝔸_f/ℤ̂* and y ∈ Y is the element all of whose components are 1. The closure F of the orbit ℕ^×y is compact, and for q ∈ ℚ* one has qy ∈ F if and only if q ∈ ±ℕ^×. The space X is noncommutative because the action of ℚ* on 𝔸_ℚ is ergodic for the Haar measure. This geometrization gives a trace-formula interpretation of the Riemann–Weil explicit formulas (see § 6 below).

The space X is also the space of points of a Grothendieck topos arising from the action of ℕ^× by multiplication on [0, ∞) [86, 87]. Furthermore, X is the space of points of the arithmetic site defined over the semifield ℝ₊^max. The Galois group of ℝ₊^max over the Boolean semifield 𝔹 is ℝ₊^*, acting by automorphisms x ↦ x^λ. This action replaces the action of the Frobenius on points over 𝔽̄_q in finite characteristic.

The relevant limit is

$$\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(x^{1/\epsilon}+y^{1/\epsilon}\right)^{\epsilon}=\max\{x,y\}=x\vee y. \tag{30}$$

The automorphism group and Frobenius maps are

$$\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{R}_+^{\max}):=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{R}_+^{\max})=\mathbb{R}_+^*,\qquad \mathrm{Fr}_\lambda(x)=x^\lambda,\ \ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}_+^{\max},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}_+^*. \tag{31}$$

### Von Neumann algebras and factors

Factors ℳ admit a canonical time evolution, given by a canonical homomorphism

$$\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow \mathrm{Out}(\mathcal{M})=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{M})/\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{M}). \tag{1}$$

The class of the modular automorphism σ_t^φ in Out(ℳ) does not depend on the choice of the faithful normal state φ [41]. This relies on Tomita–Takesaki theory [262], which constructs the map φ ↦ σ_t^φ.

The following statements are made:

- The kernel T(ℳ) = Ker δ is a subgroup of ℝ, called the periods of ℳ. Many non-trivial non-closed subgroups arise this way.
- The modular spectrum S(ℳ) [39] is the fundamental invariant of factors. Its intersection S(ℳ) ∩ ℝ₊^* is a closed subgroup of ℝ₊^* [44].
- Type III is subdivided into type III_λ according to S(ℳ) ∩ ℝ₊^* = λ^ℤ.
- The classification of factors of type III_λ, λ ∈ [0, 1), reduces to that of type II and automorphisms:

$$\mathcal{M}=N\rtimes_\theta\mathbb{Z},\qquad N \text{ type } \mathrm{II}_\infty,\qquad \theta\in\mathrm{Aut}(N). \tag{2}$$

- For λ ∈ (0, 1), N is a factor and θ ∈ Aut(N) has module λ, meaning it scales the trace by λ.
- For type III₀, N has non-trivial center, and the restriction of θ to the center gives a flow, which was used in [42] to show the existence of a hyperfinite non-ITPFI factor.
- The case III₁ was solved by Takesaki [261] using the crossed product by ℝ₊^*. [43] is cited for the statement that III₁ remained open in the thesis.

The von Neumann algebra of a foliated manifold (V, F) is

$$\mathcal{W}(V,F):=\{(T_\ell)\mid \text{acting on } L^2(\ell)\}, \tag{3}$$

where (T_ℓ) is a bounded family parametrized by leaves ℓ, and each T_ℓ acts on the Hilbert space L²(ℓ) of square-integrable half-densities on the leaf [46]. Modulo equality almost everywhere, this is a von Neumann algebra under pointwise addition and multiplication.

The prototype example is the Anosov foliation of the sphere bundle of a Riemann surface (see § 6.2). Its von Neumann algebra is the hyperfinite factor of type III₁.

A foliation whose Godbillon–Vey class is non-vanishing has a fundamental invariant of its von Neumann algebra admitting an invariant probability measure [43, 45]. This implies that the von Neumann algebra is of type III.

Cyclic cohomology is cited as the replacement of de Rham cohomology [52, 53]. An application in [51], following S. Hurder, uses the time derivative of the cyclic cocycle, which is the transverse fundamental class, to study the flow of weights.
