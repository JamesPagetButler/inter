# Restatement of the Supplied Text

## Abstract

Pyknotic objects are (hyper)sheaves on the site of compacta. These provide a convenient way to do algebra and homotopy theory with additional topological information present. This appears, for example, when trying to contemplate the derived category of a local field. In this article, we present the basic theory of pyknotic objects, with a view to describing a simple set of everyday examples.

## 0 Introduction

### 0.1 The proétale topology and pyknotic objects

Let $E$ be a local field, and let $X$ be a connected, topologically noetherian, coherent scheme. Bhargav Bhatt and Peter Scholze [38, Lemma 7.4.7] construct a topological group $\pi_1^{\textit{proét}}(X)$ that classifies local systems of $E$-vector spaces, in the sense that there is a monodromy equivalence of categories between the continuous $E$-linear representations and $E$-linear local systems.

The group $\pi_1^{\textit{proét}}(X)$ is not profinite, nor even a proobject in discrete groups, in general: Deligne's example of a curve of genus $\geq 1$ with two points identified has local systems that are not classified by any such group.

In forthcoming work [37], we will extend the Bhatt–Scholze monodromy equivalence to an exodromy equivalence between continuous $E$-representations of the Galois category $\Gal(X)$ and constructible sheaves of $E$-vector spaces. To speak of such continuous representations, one needs to contemplate not only the category of finite-dimensional $E$-vector spaces but also the natural topology thereupon. To describe constructible sheaves of complexes of $E$-vector spaces, we need a new idea in order to speak of an $\infty$-category of perfect complexes of $E$-vector spaces in a manner that retains the natural topological information coming from $E$. In this paper, we describe a way to do this: a *pyknotic* object of an $\infty$-category $C$ is a (hyper)sheaf on the site of compact Hausdorff spaces valued in $C$. We may thus speak of pyknotic sets, pyknotic groups, pyknotic rings, pyknotic spaces, pyknotic $\infty$-categories, etc.

Pyknotic structures function in much the same way as topological structures. At the same time, pyknotic sets are the proétale sheaves of sets on a separably closed field, and the proétale topos of any coherent scheme has the natural structure of a pyknotic category. There is a deep connection between the passage from objects to pyknotic objects and the passage from the étale topology to the proétale topology.

Our local field $E$ is naturally a pyknotic ring; pyknotic vector spaces over $E$ comprise a pyknotic category; complexes of pyknotic vector spaces over $E$ comprise a pyknotic $\infty$-category $\mathbfit{D}(E)$; and perfect complexes of pyknotic vector spaces over $E$ comprise a pyknotic subcategory $\mathbfit{D}^{\textit{perf}}(E)$. Our exodromy equivalence will then be a natural equivalence
$$\Fun^{\textbf{Pyk}}(\Gal(X),\mathbfit{D}^{\textit{perf}}(E))\simeq\mathbfit{D}^{\textit{constr}}_{\textit{proét}}(X;E).$$

Moreover, the proétale $\infty$-topos $X_{\textit{proét}}$ itself is naturally a pyknotic category, and one can identify it with the category of pyknotic functors from $\Gal(X)$ to pyknotic spaces:
$$X_{\textit{proét}}\simeq\Fun^{\textbf{Pyk}}(\Gal(X),\textbf{Pyk}(\mathbfit{S})).$$

### 0.2 The aims of this paper

This paper is the first of a series. Its objective is only to establish the very basic formalism of pyknotic structures, in the interest of developing a few key examples.

**0.2.1 Example.** For any set, group, abelian group, ring, space, spectrum, category, etc., $A$, there are both a discrete pyknotic object $A^{\textit{disc}}$ and an indiscrete pyknotic object $A^{\textit{indisc}}$ attached to $A$ (2.3.6). As with topological structures, these notions are set up so that a map out of a discrete object is determined by a map at the level of the underlying object, and a map into an indiscrete object is determined by a map at the level of the underlying object.

**0.2.2 Example.** Starting with discrete objects, one can develop more interesting pyknotic structures by the formation of inverse limits. Thus profinite groups like Galois groups and étale fundamental groups are naturally pyknotic, and profinite categories like $\Gal(X)$ above are naturally pyknotic (4.3.13). These inverse limits are no longer discrete.

**0.2.3 Example.** More generally still, compactly generated topological spaces embed fully faithfully into pyknotic sets, in a manner that preserves limits (2.1.6). Thus locally compact abelian groups, normed rings, and complete locally convex topological vector spaces are all naturally pyknotic objects. This includes the vast majority of topological objects that appear in number theory and functional analysis.

One key point, however, is that the relationship between compactly generated topological spaces and pyknotic sets is dual to the relationship between compactly generated topological spaces and general topological spaces: in topological spaces, compactly generated topological spaces are stable under colimits but not limits; in pyknotic sets, compactly generated topological spaces are stable under limits but not colimits.

Furthermore, since pyknotic sets form a $1$-topos, it follows readily that products of quotients are again quotients (2.2.11). This is of course not true in the realm of topological spaces, and this is one of the main reasons that topologising fundamental groups is such a fraught endeavour.

**0.2.4 Example.** More exotically, the cokernel $\widehat{\mathbfit{Z}}/\mathbfit{Z}$ in pyknotic groups is not indiscrete. This is in contrast with the topological case. Even more dramatically, if $A$ is a locally compact abelian group, the continuous homomorphism $i\colon A^{\textit{disc}}\to A$, when viewed as a pyknotic homomorphism, is a monomorphism with a nontrivial cokernel. The underlying abelian group of this cokernel, however, is trivial. This underscores one of the main peculiarities of the theory of pyknotic structures, which is also one of its advantages: the forgetful functor is not faithful.

**0.2.5 Example.** Pyknotic spaces and spectra form well-behaved categories, and their homotopy groups are naturally pyknotic. This makes it sensible to speak of topologies on the homotopy groups of spaces and spectra. For example, the $E$-nilpotent completion of a spectrum is naturally a pyknotic spectrum (3.1.16), and its homotopy pyknotic groups are computed by the $E$-based Adams–Novikov spectral sequence.

**0.2.6 Example.** The category of pyknotic objects of a presentable category $C$ forms a natural example of a pyknotic category: the category of sections over any compactum $K$ is itself the category of sheaves in $C$ on the site of compacta over $K$. Pyknotic categories provide a context in which one can do homotopy theory while keeping control of "topological" structures. For example, for a local field $E$, one may speak of the pyknotic derived category $\mathbfit{D}_{\textbf{Pyk}}(E)$, whose objects can be thought of as complexes of pyknotic vector spaces over $E$. This construction will be the focus of our attention in a sequel to this paper.

### 0.3 Pyknotic and condensed

As we were developing these ideas, we learned that Dustin Clausen and Peter Scholze have independently been studying essentially the same notion, which they call *condensed objects*.² In fact, as we were preparing this first manuscript, Scholze's ongoing lecture notes [57] appeared and Scholze gave a talk at MSRI on this material [55, 56].

There is, however, a difference between pyknotic objects and the condensed objects of Clausen and Scholze: it is a matter of set theory. To explain this, select a strongly inaccessible cardinal $\delta$ and the smallest strongly inaccessible cardinal $\delta^{+}$ over $\delta$. A pyknotic set in the universe $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta^{+}}$ is a sheaf on the site $\textbf{Comp}_{\delta}$ of $\delta$-small compacta, valued in the category $\textbf{Set}_{\delta^{+}}$ of $\delta^{+}$-small sets. By contrast, a condensed set in the universe $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta}$ is a sheaf on $\textbf{Comp}_{\delta}$ valued in $\textbf{Set}_{\delta}$ that is in addition $\kappa$-accessible for some regular cardinal $\kappa<\delta$. Thus condensed sets in $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta}$ embed fully faithfully into pyknotic sets in $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta^{+}}$, which in turn embed fully faithfully into condensed sets in $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta^{+}}$. (We shall discuss this accessibility more precisely in section 1.4.) The Clausen–Scholze theory of condensed objects can thus be formalised completely in ZFC, whereas our theory of pyknotic objects requires at least one strongly inaccessible cardinal.

As emphasised by Scholze, however, the distinction between pyknotic and condensed does have some consequences beyond philosophical matters. For example, the indiscrete topological space $\{0,1\}$, viewed as a sheaf on the site of compacta, is pyknotic but not condensed (relative to any universe). By allowing the presence of such pathological objects into the category of pyknotic sets, we guarantee that it is a topos, which is not true for the category of condensed sets.

It would be too glib to assert that the pyknotic approach values the niceness of the category over the niceness of its objects, while the condensed approach does the opposite. However, it seems that the pyknotic objects that one will encounter in serious applications will usually be condensed, and the majority of the good properties of the category of condensed objects will usually be inherited from the category of pyknotic objects.

## 1 Conventions and Set-Theoretic Background

**1.1.1.** We use the language and tools of higher category theory, particularly in the model of quasicategories, as defined by Michael Boardman and Rainer Vogt and developed by André Joyal and Jacob Lurie. We will generally follow the terminological and notational conventions of Lurie's trilogy [32, 33, 34], with the following terminology:

- The word *category* here will always mean $\infty$-category or $(\infty,1)$-category or quasicategory, i.e., a simplicial set satisfying the weak Kan condition.
- A subcategory $C^{\prime}$ of a category $C$ is a simplicial subset that is stable under composition in the strong sense, so that if $\sigma\colon\Delta^{n}\to C$ is an $n$-simplex of $C$, then $\sigma$ factors through $C^{\prime}\subseteq C$ if and only if each of the edges $\sigma(\Delta^{\{i,i+1\}})$ does so.
- We will use the terms *groupoid* or *space* interchangeably for what is often called an $\infty$-groupoid, i.e., a category in which every morphism is invertible.
- For a category $C$, we write $\Pro(C)$ for the category of proobjects in $C$.

**1.2.1.** Recall that if $\delta$ is a strongly inaccessible cardinal (which we always assume to be uncountable), then the set $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta}$ of all sets of rank strictly less than $\delta$ is a Grothendieck universe [59, Exposé I, Appendix] of rank and cardinality $\delta$. Conversely, if $\mathbfit{V}$ is a Grothendieck universe that contains an infinite cardinal, then $\mathbfit{V}=\mathbfit{V}_{\delta}$ for some inaccessible cardinal $\delta$.

In order to deal precisely and simply with set-theoretic problems arising from some of the "large" operations, we append to ZFC the Axiom of Universes (AU). This asserts that any cardinal is dominated by a strongly inaccessible cardinal. We write $\delta_{0}$ for the smallest strongly inaccessible cardinal. Now AU implies the existence of a hierarchy of strongly inaccessible cardinals
$$\delta_{0}<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\cdots,$$
in which for each ordinal $\alpha$, the cardinal $\delta_{\alpha}$ is the smallest strongly inaccessible cardinal that dominates $\delta_{\beta}$ for any $\beta<\alpha$.⁴ We certainly will not use the full strength of AU. At the cost of some awkward circumlocutions, one could even get away with ZFC alone.

**1.2.2 Definition.** Let $\delta$ be a strongly inaccessible cardinal. A set, group, simplicial set, category, ring, etc., will be said to be *$\delta$-small*⁵ if it is equivalent (in whatever appropriate sense) to one that lies in $\mathbfit{V}_{\delta}$. We write

$$\left.\begin{aligned} \text{\emph{tiny}}&\\ \text{\emph{small}}&\end{aligned}\right\}\text{ as shorthand for }\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{\emph{$\delta_{0}$-small}}\\ &\text{\emph{$\delta_{1}$-small.}}\end{aligned}\right.$$

A category $C$ is said to be *locally $\delta$-small* if and only if, for any objects $x,y\in C$, the mapping space $\Map_{C}(x,y)$ is $\delta$-small. We write

$$\left.\begin{aligned} \text{\emph{locally tiny}}&\\ \text{\emph{locally small}}&\end{aligned}\right\}\text{ as shorthand for }\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{\emph{locally $\delta_{0}$-small}}\\ &\text{\emph{locally $\delta_{1}$-small.}}\end{aligned}\right.$$

**1.2.3.** For a strongly inaccessible cardinal $\delta$, we shall write $\mathbfit{S}_{\delta}$ for the category of $\delta$-small spaces and $\textbf{Cat}_{\delta}$ for the category of $\delta$-small categories. The categories $\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{\alpha}}$ and $\textbf{Cat}_{\delta_{\alpha}}$ are $\delta_{\alpha+1}$-small and locally $\delta_{\alpha}$-small. We write

$$\left.\begin{aligned} \mathbfit{S}&\\ \textbf{Cat}&\end{aligned}\right\}\text{ as shorthand for }\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{1}}\\ &\textbf{Cat}_{\delta_{1}}.\end{aligned}\right.$$

**1.2.4.** In the same vein, if $\delta$ is a strongly inaccessible cardinal, $\delta$-accessibility of categories and functors and $\delta$-presentability of categories will refer to accessibility and presentability with respect to some $\delta$-small cardinal. A $\delta_{\alpha}$-accessible category is always $\delta_{\alpha+1}$-small and locally $\delta_{\alpha}$-small. We write $\textbf{Pr}_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{L}\subset\textbf{Cat}_{\delta_{\alpha+1}}$ (respectively, $\textbf{Pr}_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{R}\subset\textbf{Cat}_{\delta_{\alpha+1}}$) for the subcategory whose objects are presentable categories and whose functors are left (resp., right) adjoints. We write

$$\left.\begin{aligned} \text{\emph{accessible}}&\\ \text{\emph{presentable}}&\\ \textbf{Pr}^{L}&\\ \textbf{Pr}^{R}&\end{aligned}\right\}\text{ as shorthand for }\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{\emph{$\delta_{1}$-accessible}}\\ &\text{\emph{$\delta_{1}$-presentable}}\\ &\textbf{Pr}_{\delta_{1}}^{L}\\ &\textbf{Pr}_{\delta_{1}}^{R}\end{aligned}\right.$$

Accordingly, a $\delta$-topos is a left exact accessible localisation of a functor category $\Fun(C,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta})$ for some $\delta$-small category $C$. We write *topos* as a shorthand for *$\delta_{1}$-topos*.

**1.3.1 Definition.** A site $(C,\tau)$ consists of a category $C$ equipped with a Grothendieck topology $\tau$.

**1.3.2 Notation.** Let $\delta$ be a strongly inaccessible cardinal. We write $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}(C)_{\delta}\subseteq\Fun(C^{\textit{op}},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta})$ for the full subcategory spanned by the sheaves on $C$ with respect to the topology $\tau$. We write $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}^{\textit{hyp}}(C)_{\delta}\subset\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}(C)_{\delta}$ for the full subcategory spanned by the hypercomplete sheaves.⁶ In particular, we write $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}(C)$ and $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}^{\textit{hyp}}(C)$ as shorthand for $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}(C)_{\delta_{1}}$ and $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}^{\textit{hyp}}(C)_{\delta_{1}}$, respectively.

⁶ For background on hypercompleteness, see [32, §6.5].

**1.3.3 Warning.** Let $(C,\tau)$ be a site. Assume that for some object $X\in C$, there does not exist a tiny set of covering sieves of $X$ that is cofinal among all covering sieves.⁷ Then the sheafification of a tiny presheaf on $C$ (i.e., a presheaf $C^{\textit{op}}\to\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}$) might no longer be tiny. The point is that sheafification will involve a colimit over all covering sieves. As a consequence, the category $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}(C)_{\delta_{0}}$ of tiny sheaves on $C$ is not a $\delta_{0}$-topos. This is a perennial bugbear, for example, with the fpqc topology on the category of affine schemes. The sites $(C,\tau)$ with which we will be working suffer from this as well. Some authors simply elect never to sheafify a presheaf with respect to such topologies. However, in this article, we will be unable to avoid sheafification, and we do not wish to pass artificially to a subcategory of $C$, so we will permit ourselves the luxury of "universe hopping": in our cases of interest, $C$ will be small (but not tiny), and so $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau}(C)$ is a left exact localisation of $\Fun(C^{\textit{op}},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{1}})$ and thus a $\delta_{1}$-topos. In section 1.4, we outline a proof that when the site is suitably accessible, then the sheafification of the small sheaves that arise in practice are again small. This is an adaptation of the strategy developed by Waterhouse [60]. This gives a slightly more conservative way to deal with this issue.

⁷ So, in particular, $C$ itself is not tiny.

**1.3.4 Definition.** A site $(C,\tau)$ is said to be *finitary* if and only if $C$ admits all finite limits, and, for every object $X\in C$ and every covering sieve $R\subseteq C_{/X}$, there is a finite subset $\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq R$ that generates a covering sieve.

**1.3.5 Definition.** A *presite* is a pair $(C,E)$ consisting of a category $C$ along with a subcategory $E\subseteq C$ satisfying the following conditions.
- The subcategory $E$ contains all equivalences of $C$.
- The category $C$ admits finite limits, and $E$ is stable under base change.
- The category $C$ admits finite coproducts, which are universal, and $E$ is closed under finite coproducts.

**1.3.6 Construction.** If $(C,E)$ is a presite, then there exists a topology $\tau_{E}$ on $C$ in which the $\tau_{E}$-covering sieves are generated by finite families $\{V_{i}\to U\}_{i\in I}$ such that $\coprod_{i\in I}V_{i}\to U$ lies in $E$ [34, Proposition A.3.2.1]. The site $(C,\tau_{E})$ is finitary. We simplify notation and write $\textbf{Sh}_{E}(C)\subseteq\Fun(C^{\textit{op}},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{1}})$ for the full subcategory spanned by the small $\tau_{E}$-sheaves. Note that $\textbf{Sh}_{E}(C)$ is a topos if $C$ is small.

If in addition the coproducts in $C$ are disjoint, then a sheaf for $\tau_{E}$ valued in a category $D$ with all limits is a functor $F\colon C^{\textit{op}}\to D$ that carries finite coproducts in $C$ to finite products in $D$, and for any morphism $V\to U$ of $E$, the Čech nerve $\check{C}_{\ast}(V/U)\colon\mathbfit{\Delta}_{+}^{\textit{op}}\to C$ induces an equivalence
$$X(U)\xrightarrow{\sim}\lim_{n\in\mathbfit{\Delta}}X(\check{C}_{n}(V/U))$$
[34, Proposition A.3.3.1]. In this case, the topology $\tau_{E}$ is subcanonical.

**1.4.1 Definition.** Let $\beta$ be a tiny regular cardinal. A presite $(C,E)$ is said to be *$\beta$-accessible* if and only if the following conditions hold.
- Coproducts in $C$ are disjoint.
- The opposite $C^{\textit{op}}$ is $\beta$-accessible. We write $C_{\beta}\subseteq C$ for the tiny category of $\beta$-cocompact objects (i.e., objects that are $\beta$-compact as objects of $C^{\textit{op}}$).
- Every morphism $X^{\prime}\to X$ of $E$ can be exhibited as a limit of a diagram $\mathit{\Lambda}^{\textit{op}}\to\Fun(\mathit{\Delta}^{1},E\cap C_{\beta})$ in which $\mathit{\Lambda}$ is $\beta$-filtered.

We say that a small presite $(C,E)$ is *accessible* if and only if $(C,E)$ is $\beta$-accessible for some tiny regular cardinal $\beta$.

**1.4.2.** Let $\beta$ be a tiny regular cardinal, and let $(C,E)$ be a $\beta$-accessible presite. Write $E_{\beta}\coloneqq E\cap C_{\beta}$; then $(C_{\beta},E_{\beta})$ is a tiny presite (in which coproducts are still disjoint). Consequently, $\textbf{Sh}_{E_{\beta}}(C_{\beta})_{\delta_{0}}$ is a $\delta_{0}$-topos.

**1.4.3 Proposition.** Let $\beta$ be a tiny regular uncountable cardinal, and let $(C,E)$ be a $\beta$-accessible presite. Let $f\colon C_{\beta}^{\textit{op}}\to\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}$ be a functor, and let $F\colon C^{\textit{op}}\to\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}$ be the left Kan extension of $f$. Then $f$ is a $\tau_{E_{\beta}}$-sheaf if and only if $F$ is a $\tau_{E}$-sheaf.

**1.4.4.** Let $(C,E)$ be an $\omega$-accessible presite. If $N$ is a natural number, then a functor $f\colon C_{\beta}^{\textit{op}}\to\tau_{\leq N}\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}$ is a $\tau_{E_{\beta}}$-sheaf if and only if its left Kan extension $F\colon C^{\textit{op}}\to\tau_{\leq N}\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}$ is a $\tau_{E}$-sheaf. The truncatedness assumption ensures that the limit over $\mathbfit{\Delta}$ can be replaced with a limit over the full subcategory $\mathbfit{\Delta}_{\leq N+1}$ of totally ordered finite sets of cardinality at most $N+2$, which is finite. This permits us to commute the filtered colimit past the totalisation.

**1.4.5 Corollary.** Let $\beta$ be a tiny, regular, uncountable cardinal, and let $(C,E)$ be a $\beta$-accessible presite. The left Kan extension defines an equivalence of categories between the topos $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau_{E_{\beta}}}(C_{\beta})_{\delta_{0}}$ and the full subcategory of $\textbf{Sh}_{\tau_{E}}(C)_{\delta_{0}}$ spanned by the $\beta$-accessible sheaves.

**1.4.6 Notation.** If $(C,E)$ is an accessible presite, then we write $\textbf{Sh}_{E}^{\textit{acc}}(C)_{\delta_{0}}\subseteq\Fun(C^{\textit{op}},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$ for the full subcategory spanned by the accessible sheaves. More generally, if $D$ is any $\delta_{0}$-presentable category, then $\textbf{Sh}_{E}^{\textit{acc}}(C;D)\subseteq\Fun(C^{\textit{op}},D)$ is the full subcategory spanned by the accessible sheaves.

**1.4.7 Corollary.** Let $(C,E)$ be an accessible presite, and let $D$ be a $\delta_{0}$-presentable category. Then $\textbf{Sh}_{E}^{\textit{acc}}(C;D)$ is a left exact localisation of the category $\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(C^{\textit{op}},D)$ of accessible functors $C^{\textit{op}}\to D$.

**1.4.8 Example.** If $C$ is a tiny regular disjunctive category, then $\Pro^{\delta_{0}}(C)$ is an accessible presite with its effective epimorphism topology.

**1.4.9 Warning.** If $(C,E)$ is an accessible presite that is not tiny, then $\textbf{Sh}_{E}^{\textit{acc}}(C)_{\delta_{0}}$ cannot be expected to be a $\delta_{0}$-topos, or even $\kappa$-accessible with respect to a tiny cardinal $\kappa$. It is however locally tiny, and it does have many of the good features enjoyed by $\delta_{0}$-topoi. For convenience, we formalise the situation.

**1.4.10 Definition.** Let $D$ be an accessible category, and let $L\colon\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\mathbfit{X}\subseteq\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$ be a localisation. For any small regular cardinal $\alpha$, if $D$ is $\alpha$-accessible, then let us write $\mathbfit{X}_{\alpha}$ for the essential image of $L$ restricted to $\Fun(D_{\alpha},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\simeq\Fun^{\alpha\text{-}\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$. Equivalently,
$$\mathbfit{X}_{\alpha}\simeq\mathbfit{X}\cap\Fun(D_{\alpha},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$$
inside $\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$.

We shall say that the localisation functor $L$ is *macroaccessible* if for any small cardinal $\beta$, there exists a small regular cardinal $\alpha>\beta$ such that $D$ is $\alpha$-accessible, and $L$ restricts to an accessible functor
$$L_{\alpha}\colon\Fun(D_{\alpha},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\mathbfit{X}_{\alpha}\subseteq\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D_{\alpha},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}).$$

A *macropresentable category* is a category $\mathbfit{X}$ such that there exists an accessible category $D$ and a macroaccessible localisation $L\colon\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$ whose essential image is equivalent to $\mathbfit{X}$. A *macrotopos* is a category $\mathbfit{X}$ such that there exists an accessible category $D$ and a left exact, macroaccessible localisation
$$L\colon\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\mathbfit{X}\subseteq\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}).$$

**1.4.11.** If $\mathbfit{X}$ is a macropresentable category, then $\mathbfit{X}$ is the macroaccessible localisation of $\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$ for an accessible category $D$; write $L\colon\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\mathbfit{X}\subseteq\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D,\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})$ for the localisation functor. If $\alpha<\beta$ are regular cardinals with the properties that $D$ is both $\alpha$- and $\beta$-accessible and that $L$ restricts to accessible functors
$$L_{\alpha}\colon\Fun(D_{\alpha},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\Fun^{\textit{acc}}(D_{\alpha},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\beta}\colon\Fun(D_{\beta},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}})\to\Fun(D_{\beta},\mathbfit{S}_{\delta_{0}}),$$
then we have an inclusion $\mathbfit{X}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathbfit{X}_{\beta}$. The macropresentable category $\mathbfit{X}$ is the $\delta_{1}$-small filtered colimit of the presentable categories $\mathbfit{X}_{\alpha}$ under fully faithful left adjoints. Similarly, if $\mathbfit{X}$ is a macrotopos, then the $\mathbfit{X}_{\alpha}$ are topoi, and so $\mathbfit{X}$ is a $\delta_{1}$-small filtered union of topoi under fully faithful left exact left adjoints.

**1.4.12 Example.** If $(C,E)$ is an accessible presite, then $\textbf{Sh}^{\textit{acc}}_{E}(C)_{\delta_{0}}$ is a macrotopos.

**2.1.1.** Let $\textbf{TSpc}$ denote the category of tiny topological spaces. Write $\textbf{Comp}\subset\textbf{TSpc}$ for the full subcategory spanned by the compacta, i.e., tiny compact Hausdorff topological spaces. We write $\beta\colon\textbf{TSpc}\to\textbf{Comp}$ for the left adjoint to the inclusion, given by Stone–Čech compactification. The category $\textbf{Comp}$ can be identified with the category of $\beta$-algebras on $\textbf{Set}_{\delta_{0}}$, where $\beta\colon\textbf{Set}_{\delta_{0}}\to\textbf{Set}_{\delta_{0}}$ is the ultrafilter monad [45, Chapter III, §2.4].

**2.1.2.** Since the category $\textbf{Comp}$ of compacta is a $1$-pretopos, $\textbf{Comp}$ comes equipped with the *effective epimorphism topology*; a collection of morphisms $\{U_{i}\to U\}_{i\in I}$ is a cover if and only if there exists a finite subset $I_{0}\subset I$ such that the map $\coprod_{i\in I_{0}}U_{i}\twoheadrightarrow U$ is a surjection (= effective epimorphism in $\textbf{Comp}$). Note that 1.3.6 gives a complete characterisation of sheaves on $\textbf{Comp}$; see also [50, Proposition B.5.5].

**2.1.3 Definition.** The category of *pyknotic sets* is the category $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})\coloneqq\textbf{Sh}_{\textit{eff}}(\textbf{Comp};\textbf{Set})$ of small sheaves of sets on $\textbf{Comp}$ with respect to the effective epimorphism topology.

**2.1.4.** The category $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ is a coherent $1$-topos. By the classification theorem for coherent $1$-topoi [50, Theorem C.6.5], the coherent objects of $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ are exactly the compacta, regarded as representables.

**2.1.5 Notation.** The $1$-category $\textbf{CG}$ of compactly generated topological spaces is the smallest full subcategory of the category $\textbf{TSpc}_{\delta_{1}}$ of small topological spaces containing $\textbf{Comp}$ and closed under small colimits. In particular, $\textbf{CG}$ is a colocalisation of $\textbf{TSpc}_{\delta_{1}}$.

**2.1.6 Example.** Let $X$ be a small topological space. Then the functor $\Mor_{\textbf{TSpc}_{\delta_{1}}}(-,X)\colon\textbf{Comp}^{\textit{op}}\to\textbf{Set}$ is a pyknotic set. We can endow the underlying set of $X$ with the induced topology with respect to the class of continuous morphisms from compacta. This is as coarse as the topology on $X$, and coincides with the topology on $X$ if and only if $X$ is compactly generated. In other words, the Yoneda embedding extends to a functor $j\colon\textbf{TSpc}\to\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ with a left adjoint defined by left Kan extension of the inclusion $\textbf{Comp}\hookrightarrow\textbf{TSpc}$ along $\textbf{Comp}\hookrightarrow\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$. The counit of this adjunction is a homeomorphism on compactly generated topological spaces, and so the Yoneda embedding defines a fully faithful functor from compactly generated topological spaces into pyknotic sets; this expresses the category $\textbf{CG}$ as a localisation of $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$. This is one important way in which topological spaces are different from pyknotic sets: compactly generated topological spaces are not stable under colimits in $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$. Notationally, we'll often ignore the distinction between a compactly generated topological space and its corresponding pyknotic set.

**2.1.7 Lemma.** Let $X_{0}\to X_{1}\to\cdots$ be a sequence of compactly generated topological spaces. Assume that the colimit $\colim_{n}X_{n}$ in $\textbf{TSpc}_{\delta_{1}}$ is a $T_{1}$ topological space. Then the natural morphism
$$\colim_{n\geq 0}j(X_{n})\to j\!\left(\colim_{n\geq 0}X_{n}\right)$$
is an equivalence in $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$.

**2.1.8 Warning.** Note that [48, Appendix A, Lemma 9.4], used in the proof of 2.1.7, does not hold for more general filtered colimits: the unit interval is the filtered colimit of all of its countable subspaces, but the identity map does not factor through a countable subspace.

**2.1.9 Example.** The category $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ is compactly generated, and the Yoneda embedding $\textbf{Comp}\hookrightarrow\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ carries compacta to compact objects of the category $\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ [34, Corollary A.2.3.2]. Thus the Yoneda embedding extends to a fully faithful embedding $\Ind(\textbf{Comp})\hookrightarrow\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set})$ [32, Proposition 5.3.5.11]. Regarding profinite sets as Stone topological spaces under Stone duality, we thus obtain an embedding
$$\Ind(\Pro(\textbf{Set}^{\,\textit{fin}}))\hookrightarrow\textbf{Pyk}(\textbf{Set}).$$
Ind-profinite sets and extensions to ind-pro-$\cdots$-ind-profinite sets have been exploited by Kato in studying higher local fields [47], as well as by Mazel-Gee–Peterson–Stapleton in homotopy theory [53, §2]. In particular, local fields of dimension at most $1$ may be understood in terms of ind-profinite sets.
