# Restatement

## Abstract

Shulman's spatial type theory internalizes the modalities of Lawvere's axiomatic cohesion in homotopy type theory, which allows many constructions from Schreiber's modal approach to differential cohomology to be carried out synthetically. In spatial type theory, every type carries a spatial cohesion among its points, and every function is continuous with respect to it.

Objects may be spatial in more than one way at once. For example, a simplicial space has both topological and simplicial structure. This paper proposes a type theory with "commuting focuses" that allows types to carry multiple kinds of spatial structure. The theory extends spatial type theory and gives a synthetic account of simplicial, differential, equivariant, and other cohesions carried by the same types.

The theory is applied to show that the homotopy type of any differential stack may be computed from a discrete simplicial set derived from the Čech nerve of any good cover. Other examples of commuting cohesions are given, including differential equivariant types and supergeometric types. These examples lay groundwork for a synthetic account of Schreiber and Sati's proper orbifold cohomology.

## Introduction

**Identification in homotopy type theory.** In homotopy type theory, every mathematical object has a type. For objects $x, y : X$, the definition of the type $X$ specifies what it means to identify $x$ with $y$. For real vector spaces, an identification is a $\mathbb{R}$-linear isomorphism. For smooth manifolds, it is a diffeomorphism. For numbers, it is an equality. Homotopy theory studies such identifications.

**Topological and higher inductive circles.** The higher inductive circle $S^1$ is the type freely generated by a point with a self-identification. The topological circle $\mathbb{S}^1$ is defined as
$$\mathbb{S}^{1}\equiv\{(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}=1\}.$$
The base point of $S^1$ has non-trivial self-identifications. Two points of $\mathbb{S}^1$ are identified uniquely when they are equal. The higher inductive circle $S^1$ is the homotopy type of $\mathbb{S}^1$, obtained by identifying points along continuous deformations. Ordinary homotopy type theory cannot express this relationship, so it cannot transfer synthetic theorems about $S^1$ to topological questions about $\mathbb{S}^1$. A way is needed to distinguish types carrying topological structure from discrete types with only homotopical structure.

**Lawvere's axiomatic cohesion.** Lawvere formalized the opposition between cohesion and discreteness as an adjoint triple of toposes [lawvere:cohesion]:
$$\text{Mengen} \underset{\text{codiscrete}}{\overset{\text{points}}{\rightleftarrows}} \text{Kardinalen},\qquad \text{Kardinalen} \underset{\text{discrete}}{\overset{}{\rightleftarrows}} \text{Mengen}$$
The left adjoint $\flat$ retopologizes a space with the discrete topology. The right adjoint $\sharp$ retopologizes it with the codiscrete topology. When spaces are locally connected, a fourth adjoint $\pi_0$ on the left produces the discrete set of connected components. This system characterizes axiomatic cohesion.

**Cohesion in $\infty$-toposes.** Schreiber's move from 1-toposes to $\infty$-toposes [urs:diff-coh] replaces $\pi_0$ with $\Pi_\infty$, which takes the shape of a stack in the sense of Lurie [lurie:htt, §7.1.6]. A cohesive $\infty$-logos has three adjoint endofunctors $\shape \dashv \flat \dashv \sharp$. Here $\shape$ takes the homotopy type of a higher space regarded as discrete, $\flat$ takes its underlying homotopy type of discrete points, and $\sharp$ takes the underlying homotopy type of points retopologized codiscretely. Interpreting Lawvere's axioms in $\infty$-toposes yields the hexagonal fracture diagrams that characterize differential cohomology [urs:diff-coh].

**Spatial type theory.** Shulman brought this distinction into homotopy type theory via spatial type theory [mike:real-cohesive-hott]. This theory internalizes the $\flat$ and $\sharp$ modalities of [urs:diff-coh]. Earlier axiomatizations of these modalities appear in [schreiber-shulman:cohesive]. Spatial type theory replaces axioms with judgemental rules. Cohesive homotopy type theory is spatial type theory together with an additional axiom implying local contractibility. From this axiom, the further left adjoint to $\flat$ may be defined.

Homotopy type theory may be interpreted into any $\infty$-topos [kapulkin-lumsdaine:simplicial, mike:all]. Under this interpretation, a type becomes a sheaf of homotopy types. Interpreting the topological circle $\mathbb{S}^1$ into sheaves on continuous manifolds gives the sheaf represented by the external manifold $\mathbb{S}^1$. The higher inductive circle $S^1$ is interpreted as the constant sheaf at the homotopy type of the circle. By the Yoneda lemma, any function definable on $\mathbb{S}^1$ is continuous.

**Crisp variables.** Functions $f : X \to Y$ in homotopy type theory are given by terms $f(x) : Y$ in a free variable $x : X$. Variation in a free variable therefore gives a liminal continuity. Spatial type theory avoids this by introducing crisp free variables. An expression depends on its crisp free variables discontinuously. The modalities $\flat$ and $\sharp$ represent crisp variables universally on the left and right respectively. Thus $\flat X$ is the discrete retopologization of $X$, and $\sharp X$ is its codiscrete retopologization. A map out of $\flat X$ is a discontinuous map out of $X$, and a map into $\sharp X$ is a discontinuous map into $X$.

Spatial type theory is interpreted in local geometric morphisms $\gamma : \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{S}$ of $\infty$-toposes, where $\gamma^*$ has a fully faithful right adjoint $\gamma^!$. This gives a geometric morphism $f : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{E}$, with $f^* := \gamma^!$, adjoint to $\gamma$. The focal point of $\mathcal{E}$ over $\mathcal{S}$ is the object $\mathcal{S}$. The modalities $\flat$ and $\sharp$ are interpreted as the adjoint idempotent (co)monad pairs $\gamma^*\gamma_*$ and $\gamma^!\gamma_*$. A variable is crisp when it is in focus.

**Multiple spatialities.** Spaces may carry smooth, analytic, algebraic, condensed, and simplicial or cubical combinatorial structure, among others. Each gives a spatial type theory. Many cases need several axes of spatiality at once. For example, the homotopy type of a manifold may be computed as the realization of a topologically discrete simplicial set associated to the Čech nerve of a good open cover. This relates a simplicial set to a continuous space via an intermediary simplicial space that is both continuous and simplicial. Spatial homotopy type theory has only one notion of crisp variable, so it cannot express this directly.

For simplicial types, the discrete reflection is the $0$-skeleton $\sk$, and the codiscrete reflection is the $0$-coskeleton $\csk$. For simplicial spaces, there are both the topologically discrete $\flat$ and codiscrete $\sharp$, and the simplicially $0$-skeletal $\sk$ and $0$-coskeletal $\csk$. The Čech nerve of a map $f : X \to Y$ between $0$-skeletal types is its $\csk$-image. A simplicial space has a shape $\shape X$, a topologically discrete simplicial type, and a realization $\geomreal X$, a $0$-skeletal but spatial type. These modalities yield the good-cover theorem described above.

**Proper orbifold cohomology.** Sati and Schreiber's proper orbifold cohomology [sati-schreiber:proper-orbifold-cohomology] treats orbifolds as having both differential structure and global equivariant structure. They work in the $\infty$-topos of global equivariant differential stacks, which is local over both the global equivariant topos and the topos of differential stacks. The differential modalities $\flat$ and $\sharp$ are augmented with the equivariant modalities of [rezk:global-cohesion]: $\mathrel{<\!\!<}$, $\subset$, and $\prec$. These take the strict quotient, the underlying space as an invariant type, and the Yoneda embedding of the underlying space, respectively. The ordinary Borel cohomology of a global quotient orbifold $X\sslash G$ is the cohomology of $\subset(X\sslash G)$. Its proper equivariant Bredon cohomology is the cohomology of $\prec(X\sslash G)$, twisted by the map to $\prec \mathbf{B}G$ classifying the quotient map $\prec X \to \prec(X\sslash G)$. Modalities in the same position of their adjoint chain commute, so $\flat$ commutes with $\sk$ and $\sharp$ commutes with $\csk$.

**Nested focuses.** In supergeometry, as formulated by Schreiber [urs:diff-coh] with solid cohesion, the modalities are nested. The supergeometric focus is given by the even comodality $\rightrightarrows$, which takes the even part of a superspace. The rheonomic modality $\supersharp$ localizes at the odd line $\mathbb{R}^{0\mid 1}$.

**Contribution.** The paper modifies spatial type theory to allow multiple axes of spatiality. Focuses form a meet semi-lattice $\heartsuit, \clubsuit, \dots$. Each focus has its own notion of crispness and a pair of adjoint (co)modalities $\flat_V$ and $\sharp_V$. The context structure is more complex than the crisp and cohesive zones of spatial type theory. Each variable is annotated with the focuses for which it is crisp. The typing rules for each focus's modalities work essentially independently, except that $\flat$-elimination is upgraded to preserve the crispness of the eliminated term in the bound variable (a "crisp" induction principle).

The theory is compared with existing modal type theories [lsr:multi, mtt, mtt-fitch, atkey:qtt, nvd:parametric-quantifiers, mike:real-cohesive-hott, drats, grtt, cavallo:thesis]. The paper states that none of them combines dependent types that may depend on modal variables, multiple commuting comodalities each with a right adjoint, and no further left adjoints.

The theory formalizes the Čech nerve theorem, the equivariant differential cohesion of [sati-schreiber:proper-orbifold-cohomology], and the nested focuses of [urs:diff-coh]. It extends the work of Cherubini [Cherubini:Thesis] and the first author [jaz:good-fibrations, jaz:modal-fracture, jaz:orbifolds].

**Simplicial types.** Simplicial types are not yet definable in ordinary homotopy type theory. Several approaches extend the underlying type theory [rs:synthetic-cats, buccholtz-weinberger:fibered.cats, weinberger:cartesian.fibrations, weinberger:internal.sums]. The paper axiomatizes the $1$-simplex $\Delta[1]$ as a linear order with distinct top and bottom, and uses the cohesive modalities to define Čech nerves and realizations. The $\sk$ modality would take the core of a Rezk type.

**Outline.** Section 2 presents the type theory. Section 3 specializes the spatiality of a focus. Test spaces $G_i$ detect continuity when codiscreteness is detected by unique lifting against the counits $\flat G_i \to G_i$. Example test spaces are $\Delta[n]$ in the simplicial case, $\mathbb{R}^n$ in the real-cohesive case, and profinite sets for condensed sets. Section 3 also covers axioms for locally contractible spatialities. A space is locally contractible when it has constant shape in the sense of [lurie:htt, §7.1.6]. A space $C$ is contractible when every map $C \to S$ to a discrete space $S$ is constant. A space $S$ is discrete when every map $C \to S$ is constant. In that case, $C$ detects the connectivity of spaces. Examples are $\mathbb{R}$ for continuous $\infty$-groupoids and $\Delta[1]$ for simplicial $\infty$-groupoids. A local geometric morphism $p$ is strongly locally contractible when $p^*$ has a left adjoint $p_!$ taking the constant value of the shape. A morphism that is local and strongly locally contractible is called cohesive, following [lawvere:cohesion, urs:diff-coh, mike:real-cohesive-hott].

Section 3 also gives axioms for real cohesion [mike:real-cohesive-hott], simplicial cohesion, and the $\csk$ modality for Čech nerves. It covers global equivariant cohesion [rezk:global-cohesion, sati-schreiber:proper-orbifold-cohomology] and topological focuses such as the topological topos of sequential spaces [johnstone:topological-topos] and the condensed and pyknotic toposes [clausen-sholze:condensed, barwick-haine:pyknotic].

Section 4 defines orthogonal cohesions: two cohesions are orthogonal when the family detecting the connectivity of one is discrete with respect to the other, and vice versa. It proves lemmas on when modalities commute past each other. Section 5 treats examples. Simplicial real cohesion has a simplicial focus and a real-cohesive focus that are orthogonal. The shape of any $0$-skeletal type $M$ is computed as the realization of a topologically discrete simplicial type built from the Čech nerve of a good cover $U$ of $M$, one whose finite intersections are contractible in the sense of being $(-1)$-connected. Equivariant cohesion is combined with differential cohesion to give the modalities of [sati-schreiber:proper-orbifold-cohomology]; no extra axioms are needed for orthogonality. Solid cohesion extends real cohesion with the odd line $\mathbb{R}^{0\mid 1}$. Here the discrete comodality takes the even part, and the codiscrete modality takes the rheonomic reflection. Unlike the other examples, the differential focus is included in the supergeometric focus.

## Remarks, Theorems, and Definitions

**Remark 2.0.1.** Given a context $\Gamma, x :_V A\ \mathsf{ctx}$, $A$ depends only on the variables in $\Gamma$ that are themselves $V$-crisp. This context formation rule replaces the division of the context into two zones in Shulman's spatial type theory. In the conclusion of the variable rule, the type $A$ is well-formed in context $\Gamma, x :_V A, \Gamma' \ \mathsf{ctx}$ by the admissible divide-wk rule given below, followed by further weakening with $\Gamma'$.

**Remark 2.0.2.** One may be tempted to use a floating context separator $\mid_V$ for each focus, so that the variables to the left of $\mid_V$ are precisely the $V$-crisp ones. Such contexts are not sufficiently general. In particular, the $\flat$-elimination rule produces a context containing $x :_V A, y :_T B$, which cannot be separated in this way.

**Remark 2.0.3.** These rules are stronger than those used by Shulman for spatial type theory, even for a single focus. A $T$-crisp induction principle is built in for $\flat_V$, for any two focuses $V$ and $T$. If the term being inducted on is already $T$-crisp, the crispness is maintained in the new assumption $u$. With a single non-trivial focus $V$, taking $T = V$ yields the crisp $\flat$-induction principle of [mike:real-cohesive-hott, Lemma 5.1]. That principle is proven there by a detour through $\sharp$; here it is built into the rule directly. The elimination rule is also admissible from the less general rule requiring the freshly bound variable to be only $V$-crisp, but the more general rule is chosen for convenience.

**Remark 2.0.4.** The shape of the $\sharp$-form and $\sharp$-intro rules builds the left-exactness of $\flat$ into the theory. This holds already in ordinary spatial type theory. The promotion operation $V\Gamma$ distributes over context extensions in $\Gamma$, rather than being a stuck context former applied to $\Gamma$ as a whole. When $\sharp$ is used to derive crisp $\mathsf{Id}$-induction, one applies $\sharp$ to a type $x::A, y::A, p::(x=y)\mid\cdot\vdash C\ \mathsf{type}$, yielding $\cdot\mid x:A, y:A, p:(x=y)\vdash \sharp C\ \mathsf{type}$. Internally, the former context represents $(x:\flat A)\times(y:\flat A)\times\flat(x_\flat=y_\flat)$. The $\sharp$-form rule treats it as identical to $\flat((x:A)\times(y:A)\times(x=y))$ when applying adjointness.

**Remark 2.0.5.** In most cases of interest, the commutative idempotent monoid of focuses is freely generated by a finite set of basic focuses. In this situation, it suffices to provide $\flat$ and $\sharp$ only for the basic focuses, since the rest can be derived. The top focus $\top$ corresponds semantically to the entire topos. Both $\flat_\top A$ and $\sharp_\top A$ are canonically equivalent to $A$. For focuses $V$ and $T$, $\flat_{VT}$ is equivalent to $\flat_V\flat_T$, and similarly for $\sharp$.

**Theorem 3.0.1.** Any lemma or theorem proven in §3, 4, 5, and 6 of Real Cohesion [mike:real-cohesive-hott] that concerns $\flat$ and $\sharp$ and uses no axioms also holds for $\flat_V$ and $\sharp_V$, for any fixed focus $V$. Theorems that use axioms are also valid, provided the crispness used in those axioms is interpreted as $V$-crispness.
