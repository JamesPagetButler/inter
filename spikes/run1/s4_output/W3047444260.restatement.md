# Restatement

## Abstract

The concept of orbifolds should unify differential geometry with equivariant homotopy theory, and orbifold cohomology should unify differential cohomology with proper equivariant cohomology theory. The paper states that a formulation of a general theory of orbifolds reflecting this unification has remained an open problem, and it claims to present a natural theory achieving this. The paper gives:

- a general abstract axiomatization in higher topos theory;
- concrete models for ordinary orbifolds, for super-geometric orbifolds, and for higher-geometric orbifolds.

**First main result.** A fully faithful embedding of the 2-category of orbifolds into a singular-cohesive $\infty$-topos. Its intrinsic cohomology theory is proper globally equivariant differential generalized cohomology. This subsumes traditional orbifold cohomology, Chen-Ruan cohomology, and orbifold K-theory.

**Second main result.** A general construction of orbifold étale cohomology. The paper shows that it unifies (i) tangentially twisted cohomology of smooth but curved spaces with (ii) RO-graded proper equivariant cohomology of flat but singular spaces.

**Example.** $J$-twisted orbifold Cohomotopy theories with coefficients in shapes of generalized Tate spheres. According to "Hypothesis H", this includes the proper orbifold cohomology theory that controls non-perturbative string theory.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

**Orbifolds.** Orbifolds [Sa56] [Sa57] [Th80] [Hae84] are manifolds with singularities modeled on fixed points of finite group actions. Reviews are in [MM03] [Ka08, §6] [BG08, §4] [IKZ10]. Orbifolds are used in mathematics [BLP05] [Rat06, §13] [JY11] and in theoretical physics [AMR02], notably in string theory [DHVW85] [DHVW86] [BL99] [SS19].

The homotopy theory of orbifolds is the $\infty$-category of orbifolds [Lu09a], see section 2.1. Orbifold cohomology is defined from it. The definition is subtle; see [Le08, Intro.] [IKZ10, §1].

**Orbifolds as étale stacks.** A proposal popular among Lie theorists [MP97] (see also [Mo02] [Le08] [Am12]) regards an orbifold with local charts $G_i \curvearrowright U_i$ (347) as follows:

- as the étale groupoid, which is either a Lie groupoid [MM03] [TX06] or a topological groupoid [CPRST14];
- equivalently, as the étale geometric stack, which is a differentiable or topological stack [Ca11] [Ca19] [Gi13] obtained by gluing the homotopy quotient stacks $U_i /\!/ G_i$ (17).

This proposal is modeled on Deligne-Mumford stacks [DM69] (reviewed in [Kr09]). It is made explicit in [Jo12, §8]. It extends to general étale $\infty$-stacks [Ca20] [Ca16].

**Borel versus proper equivariant cohomology.** The stated reasoning is as follows. Étale stacks capture geometric aspects through generalized sheaf cohomology [Br73], see [NSS12a]. As geometric refinements of classifying spaces, they support Borel equivariant cohomology [Tu11]. Borel cohomology is coarser than the proper equivariant cohomology that is generally relevant.

Proper equivariant cohomology is formulated in equivariant homotopy theory (reviewed in [Blu17] [May96]).[^1] It refines the homotopy-theoretic nature of Borel cohomology by the geometric ("cohesive", see section 1.2) nature of fixed loci of topological group actions (see Example 3.71). It is encoded in the category of orbits of the equivariance group, recalled in appendix B.

Examples of proper equivariant theories:

- Proper equivariant ordinary cohomology is Bredon cohomology [Br67a] [Br67b] (reviewed in [Blu17, §1.4] [tD79, §7]).
- Equivariant K-theory [Se68] [AS69] is proper equivariant by [AS04, A3.2] [FHT07, A.5] [DL98].
- Equivariant Cohomotopy [Se71] [tD79, §8] [SS19] [BSS19].

The paper states that if orbifolds are modeled only by étale stacks, their proper equivariant cohomology remains largely invisible.

[^1]: The paper follows [DHLPS19] in this terminology; see Remark 4.60 below.

**Traditional orbifold cohomology.** Let $\mathcal{X}$ be an orbifold. Write $\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\subset$}\mathcal{X}$ for the étale stack underlying it (see section 1.2). Write $\raisebox{1.0pt}{}\,\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\subset$}\mathcal{X}$ for its geometric realization or classifying space, often denoted $B\mathcal{X}$.[^2] For the global quotient orbifold of a $G$-space $X$, this is the homotopy type of the Borel construction. The paper calls $\raisebox{1.0pt}{}\,\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\subset$}\mathcal{X}$ the *Borel space* of the orbifold.

Traditional orbifold cohomology is [ALR07, p. 38] the ordinary (e.g. singular) cohomology of this Borel space, i.e. Borel cohomology:

$$
H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{trad}}\big(\mathcal{X},\,A\big)\;:=\;H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{sing}}\big(\,\raisebox{1.0pt}{}\,\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\subset$}\mathcal{X},\,A\big). \tag{1}
$$

This may be taken with any coefficients $A$, including local coefficient systems [MP99]. It is always an invariant of the Borel space alone.

For coefficient rings that invert the orders of the isotropy groups of $\mathcal{X}$, in particular for $A\in\{\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}$, the purely torsion cohomology of the finite isotropy groups vanishes. Traditional orbifold cohomology then reduces to an invariant of the shape of the coarse quotient space $\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$<$}\mathcal{X}$, denoted $|\mathcal{X}|$. This is [ALR07, Prop. 2.12]:

$$
H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{trad}}\big(\mathcal{X},\,\mathbb{Q}\big)\;\simeq\;H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{sing}}\big(\,\raisebox{1.0pt}{}\,\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$<$}\,\mathcal{X},\,\mathbb{Q}\big). \tag{2}
$$

Here $\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$<$}\mathcal{X}$ is the naive/coarse quotient space. Equation (2) was originally introduced in [Sa56, Thm. 1], following [Ba54] and reviewed in [ALR07, 2.1].

[^2]: The "esh"-symbol stands for shape [Sc13, 3.4.5] [Sh15, 9.7], following [Bo75]. For well-behaved topological spaces this is another term for the homotopy type [Lu09a, 7.1.6] [Wa17, 4.6]; see Example 3.18.

**Chen-Ruan cohomology.** Chen and Ruan introduced a new orbifold cohomology theory in [CR04], motivated by [DHVW85] [DHVW86]. According to [Cl14, p. 4,7], Chen-Ruan cohomology of an orbifold is Satake's coarse cohomology (2), typically with complex coefficients, applied to the inertia orbifold $\mathbf{Maps}(S^1,\mathcal{X})$:

$$
H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{CR}}\big(\mathcal{X}\big)\;\;\simeq\;\;H^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{trad}}\big(\mathbf{Maps}(\raisebox{1.0pt}{}S^{1},\mathcal{X}),\,\mathbb{C}\big). \tag{3}
$$

For global quotient orbifolds $\mathcal{X}=\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\prec$}(X/\!/G)$, Chen-Ruan cohomology is equivalent to proper equivariant cohomology, namely Bredon cohomology with the coefficient system

$$
A_{\mathrm{CR}}\;:\;G/H\;\longmapsto\;\mathrm{ClassFunctions}(H,\mathbb{C}). \tag{4}
$$

This was observed in [Mo02, p. 18], using [Ho90, Thm. 5.5] with [Ho90, Prop. 6.5 b)]:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{\bullet}_{\mathrm{CR}}\big(\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\prec$}(X/\!/G),\,\mathbb{C}\big)\;\simeq\;H^{\bullet}_{G}\big(X,\,A_{\mathrm{CR}}\big). \tag{5}
$$

The paper notes that the success of Chen-Ruan cohomology (surveyed in [ALR07, §4,5]) highlights the relevance of proper equivariance in orbifold cohomology.

**Orbifold K-theory.** In [AR01], the authors define orbifold K-theory as the equivariant K-theory of any global quotient presentation (see also [ARZ06] [BU09] [HW11]):

$$
K^{\bullet}\big(\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\prec$}(X/\!/G)\big)\;:=\;K^{\bullet}_{G}(X). \tag{6}
$$

[PS10, Prop. 4.1] proves that the right-hand side of (6) is independent of the choice of global quotient presentation.

The paper states that, in general, defining orbifold cohomology as equivariant cohomology of global quotient presentations, instead of giving an intrinsic definition, is unsatisfactory and needs justification.

**Global equivariant homotopy theory.** [PS10] suggested that orbifold cohomology should also capture proper equivariant cohomology. The fundamental issue is that a quotient presentation $\mathcal{X}\simeq\rotatebox[origin]{70.0}{$\prec$}(X/\!/G)$ is not intrinsic to the orbifold, similar to a choice of coordinate atlas. In equivariant cohomology theory, the equivariance group $G$ is traditionally fixed.

[Schw17, Intro.] [Schw18, p. ix-x] (details in [Ju20]) suggest that the right context is global equivariant homotopy theory [Schw18], following [HG07] and motivated by patterns in genuine equivariant stable homotopy theory [Se71] [LMS86]. There the equivariance group $G$ may vary in a prescribed class of groups. Plain global homotopy theory retains no geometric information.

**Open problem.** The paper states the open problem as setting up a theory of proper orbifold cohomology that unifies:

- (i) the higher geometric (differential, étale) aspects of orbifolds, captured by geometric $\infty$-stack theory;
- (ii) the singular (equivariant) aspects of orbifolds, captured by proper and global equivariant homotopy theory.

The paper uses higher topos theory [TV05] [Lu09a] [Re10] (more pointers in section 2.1) as the ambient foundational homotopy theory of higher geometric spaces [Sc13] [Sc19]. An $\infty$-topos is viewed externally as a collection of geometric spaces of a given flavor, which may be (a) generalized geometric or (b) higher geometric.

**(a) Generalized geometry.** This refers to what Grothendieck called functorial geometry [Gr65] (reviewed in [DG80]). In [Gr73] he urged that it supersede point-set definitions of geometric spaces. Lawvere further developed this [La86] [La91]. The basic idea is that of probing: physicists describe emergent spacetimes $\mathcal{X}$ by what classical $p$-brane sigma-models on worldvolumes $\Sigma$ detect when probing $\mathcal{X}$ [FSS13a] [FSS13b] [JSSW19].

Examples of local model categories ("charts"):

$$
\mathrm{Charts}\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
\textit{CartesianSpaces} & \text{(Def. 2.5)}\\
\textit{JetsOfCartesianSpaces} & \text{(Def. 3.22)}\\
\textit{SuperCartesianSpaces} & \text{(Def. 3.41)}\\
\textit{Singularities} & \text{(Def. 3.46)}\\
\textit{SingularCharts} & \text{(Lem. 3.60)}\\
\dots
\end{array}\right. \tag{7}
$$

For a chart category as in (7) (see Def. 3.9 below), one assigns to each $\Sigma\in\mathrm{Charts}$ the collection of probes

$$
\mathcal{X}(\Sigma)\;:=\;\big\{``\;\Sigma\rightarrow\mathcal{X}\text{ ''}\big\} \tag{8}
$$

of geometric maps into $\mathcal{X}$. The quotation marks indicate that the category in which these maps are morphisms is not yet specified.

A minimal set of consistency conditions for (8) to be collections of maps into a space $\mathcal{X}$ is as follows.

1. **Functoriality of probes.** For every morphism $\phi$ of $\mathrm{Charts}$, pre-composition by $\phi$ gives $\mathcal{X}(\phi)=$ "$(-)\circ\phi$", such that

$$
\mathcal{X}(\phi_2)\circ\mathcal{X}(\phi_1)\;\simeq\;\mathcal{X}(\phi_2\circ\phi_1). \tag{9}
$$

2. **Gluing of probes.** If $\{U_i\to\Sigma\}_{i\in I}$ is a cover of $\Sigma\in\mathrm{Charts}$, then probes of $\mathcal{X}$ by $\Sigma$ are equivalent to tuples of probes $U_i\to\mathcal{X}$ that are identified on intersections $U_i\cap U_j$, compatibly on $U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k$, and so on:

$$
\mathcal{X}(\Sigma)\;\simeq\;\Big\{\text{tuples of probes } U_i\to\mathcal{X} \text{ identified on intersections } U_i\cap U_j \text{ compatibly on } U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k \text{ etc.}\Big\}. \tag{10}
$$

In the language of topos theory [MLM92] [Joh02], (9) says $\mathcal{X}(-)$ is a presheaf on $\mathrm{Charts}$, and (10) says it is a sheaf. Hence the category of generalized geometric spaces probeable by $\mathrm{Charts}$ is the Grothendieck topos

$$
\mathrm{GeneralizedSpaces}\;:=\;\mathrm{Sheaves}\big(\mathrm{Charts}\big), \tag{11}
$$

the category of sheaves on the site of charts.

Each $\Sigma\in\mathrm{Charts}$ is regarded as a generalized space $y(\Sigma)\in\mathrm{GeneralizedSpaces}$, with probes given by morphisms of $\mathrm{Charts}$. This is the Yoneda embedding (footnote 3 below; recalled as Prop. 2.37):

$$
y(\Sigma)\;:\;\Sigma'\;\longmapsto\;\big\{\Sigma'\rightarrow\Sigma\big\}\;=:\;\mathrm{Charts}(\Sigma',\Sigma). \tag{12}
$$

The bootstrap of generalized spaces is complete if one can remove the quotation marks in (8). This holds if, for $\mathcal{X}\in\mathrm{GeneralizedSpaces}$, there is a natural equivalence

$$
\mathcal{X}(\Sigma)\;\simeq\;\big\{y(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathcal{X}\big\}\;:=\;\mathrm{GeneralizedSpaces}\big(y(\Sigma),\mathcal{X}\big). \tag{13}
$$

This is the statement of the Yoneda lemma (recalled as Prop. 2.38 below).

**(b) Higher geometry.** This refers to the refinement of generalized geometric spaces (see [FSS13a] [FSS19a] [JSSW19]). The collection of probes (8) need not be just a set. It may be a set with equivalences (gauge transformations) between its elements, with higher equivalences (higher gauge transformations) between these, and so on. Such a structure is an $\infty$-groupoid, modeled for example as a Kan simplicial set [GJ99, I.3].

For $X\in\mathrm{Sets}$ and $G$ a discrete group acting on $X$, the action groupoid (Example 2.15 below) has as objects the elements $x\in X$, with an equivalence between $x_1$ and $x_2$ for each group element carrying $x_1$ to $x_2$. Its homotopy quotient is $X/\!/G$, which is compared with the plain quotient $X/G$:

$$
X/\!/G\;\simeq\;\{\,\text{objects } x,\ \text{morphisms } g\cdot x,\ \text{higher morphisms } \dots\,\}\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad X/G\;\simeq\;\{[x],[y],[z],\dots\}
$$
