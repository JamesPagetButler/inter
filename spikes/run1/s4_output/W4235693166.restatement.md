# Restatement

## Abstract

The octonions are the largest of the four normed division algebras.

## 1 Introduction

There are exactly four normed division algebras: the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$, and the octonions $\mathbb{O}$. The octonions are nonassociative.

Hamilton's fundamental quaternion relations are
$$i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=ijk=-1.$$

Graves described an 8-dimensional normed division algebra and used it to express the product of two sums of eight perfect squares as another sum of eight perfect squares (the eight squares theorem) [52]. Degen had discovered this theorem in 1818 [28, 30].

For $A,B,C$ quaternions, $A\cdot BC=AB\cdot C=ABC$. This does not hold in general for octonions.

The unit quaternions form $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, which is the double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.

Élie Cartan described triality, the symmetry between vectors and spinors in 8-dimensional Euclidean space [17].

The Lagrangian for the classical superstring involves a relationship between vectors and spinors in Minkowski spacetime which holds only in 3, 4, 6, and 10 dimensions. The following isomorphisms hold:
$$
\begin{array}{lcl}
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}) &\cong& \mathfrak{so}(2,1)\\
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}) &\cong& \mathfrak{so}(3,1)\\
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{H}) &\cong& \mathfrak{so}(5,1)\\
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{O}) &\cong& \mathfrak{so}(9,1)
\end{array}
$$
These isomorphisms allow a spinor in one of these dimensions to be treated as a pair of elements of the corresponding division algebra.

The concept of an octonionic projective space $\mathbb{OP}^{n}$ makes sense only for $n\leq 2$.

There are 3 infinite families of classical simple Lie algebras, which come from the isometry groups of $\mathbb{RP}^{n}$, $\mathbb{CP}^{n}$ and $\mathbb{HP}^{n}$. There are also 5 exceptional simple Lie algebras. Four of them come from the isometry groups of the projective planes over $\mathbb{O}$, $\mathbb{O}\otimes\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{O}\otimes\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathbb{O}\otimes\mathbb{O}$. The remaining one is the automorphism group of the octonions.

The exceptional Jordan algebra consists of $3\times 3$ hermitian octonionic matrices. Minimal projections in this Jordan algebra correspond to points of $\mathbb{OP}^{2}$, and the automorphism group of this algebra is the same as the isometry group of $\mathbb{OP}^{2}$.

Bott computed the homotopy groups of the topological group $\mathrm{O}(\infty)$, the inductive limit of the orthogonal groups $\mathrm{O}(n)$ as $n\to\infty$. He proved the period-8 repetition (Bott periodicity):
$$\pi_{i+8}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong\pi_{i}(\mathrm{O}(\infty)).$$
He also computed the first eight homotopy groups:
$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\pi_{0}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}, & \pi_{4}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong 0,\\
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}, & \pi_{5}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong 0,\\
\pi_{2}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong 0, & \pi_{6}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong 0,\\
\pi_{3}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong\mathbb{Z}, & \pi_{7}(\mathrm{O}(\infty))\cong\mathbb{Z}.
\end{array}
$$

The nonvanishing homotopy groups occur in dimensions one less than the dimensions of $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, and $\mathbb{O}$. In a normed division algebra, left multiplication by an element of norm one defines an orthogonal transformation of the algebra, and thus an element of $\mathrm{O}(\infty)$. This gives maps from the spheres $S^{0}, S^{1}, S^{3}$ and $S^{7}$ to $\mathrm{O}(\infty)$, and these maps generate the homotopy groups in those dimensions.

There exist $n$ pointwise linearly independent smooth vector fields on the $n$-sphere only when $n+1=1,2,4,$ or $8$ [56]. This can be used to show that division algebras over the reals can only occur in these dimensions.

### 1.1 Preliminaries

A vector space is a finite-dimensional module over the field of real numbers.

An algebra $A$ is a vector space equipped with a bilinear map $m\colon A\times A\to A$ (called multiplication) and a nonzero element $1\in A$ (called the unit) such that $m(1,a)=m(a,1)=a$. Write $m(a,b)$ as $ab$. Algebras are not assumed to be associative. Real numbers are identified with elements of $A$ via $\alpha\mapsto\alpha 1$.

An algebra $A$ is a division algebra if, for $a,b\in A$ with $ab=0$, either $a=0$ or $b=0$. Equivalently, $A$ is a division algebra if left and right multiplication by any nonzero element are invertible.

A normed division algebra is an algebra $A$ that is also a normed vector space with $\|ab\|=\|a\|\|b\|$. This implies that $A$ is a division algebra and that $\|1\|=1$.

An algebra $A$ has multiplicative inverses if, for each nonzero $a\in A$, there is $a^{-1}\in A$ with $aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=1$. An associative algebra has multiplicative inverses if and only if it is a division algebra. This fails for nonassociative algebras.

Setting $i^{2}=-1+\epsilon j$ for some small nonzero real $\epsilon$, while leaving the rest of the quaternion multiplication table unchanged, gives a 4-dimensional algebra that is a division algebra without multiplicative inverses. In this algebra, the element $i$ has both right and left inverses, and these are not equal.

Levels of associativity:
- An algebra is *power-associative* if the subalgebra generated by any one element is associative.
- An algebra is *alternative* if the subalgebra generated by any two elements is associative.
- An algebra is *associative* if the subalgebra generated by any three elements is associative.

By a theorem of Emil Artin [84], an algebra $A$ is alternative if and only if, for all $a,b\in A$,
$$(aa)b=a(ab),\qquad (ab)a=a(ba),\qquad (ba)a=b(aa). \tag{1}$$
Any two of these equations imply the remaining one, so the first and last are usually taken as the definition of alternative.

The associator is the trilinear map $[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]\colon A^{3}\to A$ given by
$$[a,b,c]=(ab)c-a(bc).$$
The associator is alternating (changes sign when two arguments are exchanged, or vanishes when two arguments are equal) if and only if $A$ is alternative. Any two of equations (1) imply the third.

**Theorem 1.** $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, and $\mathbb{O}$ are the only normed division algebras.

The first theorem goes back to an 1898 paper by Hurwitz [55]. A modern proof is given in [84].

**Theorem 2.** $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, and $\mathbb{O}$ are the only alternative division algebras.

A version of the second theorem appears in an 1930 paper by Zorn [100]. For modern proofs of both theorems, see Schafer [84].

It is not true that $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathbb{O}$ are the only division algebras. The 4-dimensional division algebras without multiplicative inverses described above are counterexamples.

**Theorem 3.** All division algebras have dimension $1,2,4,$ or $8$.

This was independently proved by Kervaire [62] and Bott–Milnor [11] in 1958.
