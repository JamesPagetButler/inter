# Restatement

## Abstract

We formulate a model-independent theory of co/cartesian morphisms and co/cartesian fibrations, that is, one that resides entirely within the $\infty$-category of $\infty$-categories. We prove that this theory is compatible with the corresponding quasicategorical notions studied by Joyal and Lurie. The quasicategorical notions are model-dependent.

## 0. Introduction

**Outline and goals.** This note concerns cocartesian fibrations, cartesian fibrations, and their connection to the Grothendieck construction, in the $\infty$-categorical context.

- The ur-example of a cartesian fibration is the forgetful functor $\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}$ from the category of vector bundles to the category of manifolds. Here an object is a pair $(M,V)$ of a manifold $M$ and a vector bundle $V\downarrow M$. This functor is a cartesian fibration; see 1.5.
- The ur-example of the Grothendieck construction accounts for the equivalence between two definitions of a stack in algebraic geometry: as a functor to the category of groupoids, or as a category fibered in groupoids.

The note has two purposes.

1. It gives an informal discussion of co/cartesian fibrations and their motivation from the Grothendieck construction. This discussion assumes only an intuitive sense of the term "$\infty$-category".
2. It proves that the informal discussion is rigorous. The definitions, formulated within the $\infty$-category of $\infty$-categories, are compatible with the corresponding notions for quasicategories; see 3.3 and 3.4.¹

¹ The quasicategorical definitions are not manifestly model-independent, so the proofs of these results are nontrivial. They rely on model-categorical manipulations. Section 1 gives the informal discussion and section 3 gives the proofs. Section 2 distills section 1 into precise model-independent definitions of co/cartesian morphisms and co/cartesian fibrations.

**Why model-independence.** There are three related reasons.

(a) *Working model-independently.* The theory of $\infty$-categories is a general home for derived mathematics and is used in areas such as the geometric Langlands program and mathematical physics. In these applications, $\infty$-categories are manipulated formally, and one wants the notions studied to be accessible without model-dependent notions such as quasicategories, fibrations between them, or their simplices. For the theory of quasicategories, the definitions generally descend to the underlying $\infty$-category of the model category $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{\textup{Joyal}}$. The theory of co/cartesian fibrations is an exception, so these notions are not a priori meaningful to use model-independently. The results here provide that input. They are also the key input to a model-independent reading of [Lur14], whose work relies heavily on co/cartesian morphisms in quasicategories.

(b) *Conceptual clarity.* A model-independent definition is free of point-set or model-dependent notions. As an example, the $n$th homotopy group of a based simplicial set $(Y,y)\in s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{*}$ can be defined as the subquotient
$$\pi_{n}(Y,y)=\left.\left\{\sigma\in(\mathbb{R}Y)_{n}:\delta^{n}_{i}(\sigma)=\sigma^{\circ(n-1)}(y)\textup{ for all }0\leq i\leq n\right\}\right/\sim$$
of the set of $n$-simplices of a fibrant replacement $Y\stackrel{\approx}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}Y\twoheadrightarrow\Delta^{0}$. Here the model structure is the standard Kan–Quillen structure $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{\textup{KQ}}$, and the basepoint is induced by $\Delta^{0}\xrightarrow{y}Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}Y$. This definition obscures several features of homotopy groups:
- they are independent of the choice of fibrant replacement;
- they form a group for $n\geq 1$, and an abelian group for $n\geq 2$;
- a path between two basepoints induces a conjugation isomorphism for $n\geq 1$;
- they are corepresentable in the homotopy category $\ho(\mathcal{S}_{*})$ of pointed spaces.

The quasicategorical definitions of co/cartesian morphisms and fibrations are simpler, but they are model-dependent. So the claim that they have homotopical meaning requires proof.

(c) *Alternate models.* Model-independence also lets mathematicians working in other models of $\infty$-categories use these notions within their own context. Different model categories presenting the same $\infty$-category (for example, that of spectra) can be advantageous for different purposes. Examples include:
- $\mathcal{T}\textup{op}$-enriched categories, in geometric topology;
- $\mathcal{A}_{\infty}$-categories, in mirror symmetry;
- dg-categories, in derived algebraic geometry and homological algebra;
- Segal spaces and Segal $\Theta_{n}$-spaces, in bordism theory;
- $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$-enriched categories, in homotopy theory, both as hammock localizations of relative categories (for example, model categories) and as full subcategories of bifibrant objects of simplicial model categories.

The model categories presenting "the $\infty$-category of $\infty$-categories" are connected by Quillen equivalences [BSP]. A question in any of these models can therefore be translated into a question in $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{\textup{Joyal}}$, provided one takes co/fibrant replacements at each step to compute derived values. This procedure is cumbersome, and it can obstruct the answer, since replacements generally alter point-set features. The results here show that the quasicategorical results apply in any of these models without such translation.

**Conventions.** For general references on quasicategories, see Lurie's [Lur09] and [Lur14]. For motivation on the Grothendieck construction, including applications, see [MGa]. For details on working "within the $\infty$-category of $\infty$-categories", see [MGb, §A].

## 1. Informal discussion

**Question 1.1.** If $\mathcal{B}$ is an $\infty$-category, what is classified by a map $\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$?

**Example 1.2.** Let $(\mathcal{C},\otimes)$ be a monoidal category.

- It is encoded by a functor $\mathbf{\Delta}^{op}\xrightarrow{\textup{Bar}(\mathcal{C})_{\bullet}}\mathcal{C}\textup{at}$, namely its bar construction, viewed as a monoid object in $(\mathcal{C}\textup{at},\times)$. On objects, $\textup{Bar}(\mathcal{C})_{n}=\mathcal{C}^{\times n}$. The structure maps encode the monoidal product and the unit map $\textup{pt}_{\mathcal{C}\textup{at}}\simeq\{\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}$.

- A symmetric monoidal category $(\mathcal{C},\otimes)$ is encoded by a functor $\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\textup{at}$ from the category of finite pointed sets. It sends $T_{+}=T\sqcup\{*\}$ to $\mathcal{C}^{\times T}$. It sends a morphism $T_{+}\xrightarrow{\alpha}U_{+}$ to the functor $\mathcal{C}^{\times T}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\times U}$ given by
$$(c_{t})_{t\in T}\mapsto\left(\bigotimes_{t\in\alpha^{-1}(u)}c_{t}\right)_{u\in U},$$
where a monoidal product indexed by the empty set is the unit $\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}$. If $\alpha(t)=*$ for some $t\in T$, then $c_{t}$ does not appear in any resulting product; it is "thrown away".

  Equivalently, a symmetric monoidal category is a cocartesian fibration $\mathcal{C}^{\otimes}\xrightarrow{p}\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$. For example, write $\langle n\rangle=\{1,\ldots,n\}_{+}$. The unique map $\langle 2\rangle\xrightarrow{\mu}\langle 1\rangle$ with $\mu^{-1}(*)=\{*\}$ has cocartesian lifts $(c_{1},c_{2})\rightarrow(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$. These morphisms encode the monoidal product $\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{-\otimes-}\mathcal{C}$.

- A symmetric monoidal functor $(\mathcal{C},\otimes)\xrightarrow{F}(\mathcal{D},\boxtimes)$ is equivalent to a morphism of cocartesian fibrations over $\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$, from $p:\mathcal{C}^{\otimes}\to\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$ to $q:\mathcal{D}^{\boxtimes}\to\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$, with $F$ on the total spaces. On the fiber over $T_{+}$, the induced map is $\mathcal{C}^{\times n}\xrightarrow{F^{\times n}}\mathcal{D}^{\times n}$. Preservation of monoidal products is encoded by preservation of cocartesian morphisms. For example, the $p$-cocartesian morphism $(c_{1},c_{2})\rightarrow(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$ over $\langle 2\rangle\xrightarrow{\mu}\langle 1\rangle$ is sent to $(F(c_{1}),F(c_{2}))\rightarrow F(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$, which lies over the same morphism. Preservation means this is $q$-cocartesian, which gives a unique isomorphism $F(c_{1})\boxtimes F(c_{2})\cong F(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$ in $\mathcal{D}\cong\mathcal{D}^{\times 1}$.

  If $F$ is only lax symmetric monoidal, it still defines a morphism among cocartesian fibrations, but it need not preserve cocartesian morphisms. The image of $(c_{1},c_{2})\rightarrow(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$ is an arbitrary morphism $(F(c_{1}),F(c_{2}))\rightarrow F(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$. It admits a unique cocartesian/fiber factorization
$$(F(c_{1}),F(c_{2}))\rightarrow(F(c_{1})\boxtimes F(c_{2}))\rightarrow F(c_{1}\otimes c_{2})$$
in $\mathcal{D}^{\boxtimes}$. The fiber morphism is the structure map witnessing laxness at $c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{C}$.

- The identity $\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}\xrightarrow{\textup{id}}\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$ is a cocartesian fibration. It corresponds to the canonical symmetric monoidal structure on the terminal category $\textup{pt}_{\mathcal{C}\textup{at}}$. A commutative algebra object in $(\mathcal{C},\otimes)$ is a lax symmetric monoidal functor $\textup{pt}_{\mathcal{C}\textup{at}}\xrightarrow{A}(\mathcal{C},\otimes)$. For a lax symmetric monoidal functor $F$, functoriality of commutative algebra objects is given by composition
$$\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}\xrightarrow{A}\mathcal{C}^{\otimes}\xrightarrow{F}\mathcal{D}^{\boxtimes}\xrightarrow{q}\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$$
together with $\textup{id}_{\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}}$, as a morphism among cocartesian fibrations. Equivalently, $A$ is a section of $\mathcal{C}^{\otimes}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$, and functoriality is given by functoriality of sections.

**Remark 1.3.** In 1.2, a lax symmetric monoidal functor was not claimed to be exactly characterized as a morphism of cocartesian fibrations. This needs a further definition. A morphism $T_{+}\xrightarrow{\alpha}U_{+}$ in $\mathcal{F}\textup{in}_{*}$ is *inert* if $|\alpha^{-1}(u)|=1$ for all $u\in U$. A morphism in $\mathcal{C}^{\otimes}$ is *$p$-inert* (or simply *inert*) if it is $p$-cocartesian and lies over an inert morphism. For example, the unique map $\langle 2\rangle\xrightarrow{\rho}\langle 1\rangle$ with $\rho^{-1}(1)=\{2\}$ has $p$-cocartesian lifts $(c_{1},c_{2})\rightarrow c_{2}$. A lax symmetric monoidal functor $(\mathcal{C},\otimes)\xrightarrow{F}(\mathcal{D},\boxtimes)$ is a commutative triangle as above that preserves inert morphisms, that is, sends $p$-inert morphisms to $q$-inert morphisms.

**Remark 1.4.** The observations of 1.2 and 1.3 are the foundations of the theory of $\infty$-operads in [Lur14, Chapter 2].

**Example 1.5.** Let $\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}$ be the category of vector bundles. Its objects are pairs $(M,V)$ of a manifold $M$ and a vector bundle $V\downarrow M$. Its morphisms are commutative squares
$$\begin{array}{ccc} V & \rightarrow & W \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ M & \rightarrow & N \end{array}$$
consisting of a morphism of manifolds and a compatible morphism of total spaces of vector bundles.

The forgetful functor $\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}\xrightarrow{\textup{U}_{\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}}}\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}$ is a cartesian fibration. Let $M\xrightarrow{\varphi}N$ be a morphism in $\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}$ and $W\downarrow N$ a vector bundle. A cartesian lift is given by the pullback vector bundle $\varphi^{*}(W)\rightarrow W$, which is defined by a pullback square of underlying topological spaces
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \varphi^{*}(W) & \rightarrow & W \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ M & \xrightarrow{\varphi} & N. \end{array}$$
The fiber/cartesian factorization system says that for any vector bundle $V\downarrow M$, the square
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \hom_{\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}(M)}(V\downarrow M,\varphi^{*}(M)\downarrow M) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}}(V\downarrow M,W\downarrow N) \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \{\varphi\} & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}}(M,N) \end{array}$$
is a pullback square in $\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$. Here $\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}(M)$ denotes the fiber $\textup{U}^{-1}_{\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}}(M)$, equivalently the value at $M$ of the functor $\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}^{op}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\textup{at}$ classifying the cartesian fibration.

There is a canonical section $\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}\xrightarrow{T}\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}$ of the forgetful functor, called the tangent bundle. It sends a manifold $M$ to its tangent bundle $TM\downarrow M$. This section is not a cartesian section: it does not send morphisms of $\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}$ to cartesian morphisms. Correspondingly, it does not arise from a natural transformation $\mathcal{M}\textup{fld}^{op}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\textup{at}$ from $\textup{const}(\textup{pt}_{\mathcal{C}\textup{at}})$ to $\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}$, valued in $\mathcal{C}\textup{at}$.

Given a morphism $M\xrightarrow{\varphi}N$ of manifolds, there is a canonical map $TM\rightarrow\varphi^{*}(TN)$ in $\mathcal{V}\textup{ect}\mathcal{B}\textup{dl}(M)$. This map is not generally an isomorphism. It is an isomorphism, and $TM\rightarrow\varphi^{*}(TN)$ is a cartesian lift of $\varphi$, whenever $\varphi$ is an open embedding.

**Example 1.6.** The forgetful functor $\mathcal{T}\textup{op}\xrightarrow{\textup{U}_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}}\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$ is a cartesian fibration. Let $U\rightarrow Y$ be a morphism in $\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$, and let $\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{T}\textup{op}$ be a topological space together with an isomorphism $\textup{U}_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}(\mathcal{Y})\cong Y$ in $\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$. A $\textup{U}_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}$-cartesian lift is given by equipping the set $U$ with the induced topology. This yields a topological space $\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{T}\textup{op}$ with a map $\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}$ in $\mathcal{T}\textup{op}$ and an isomorphism $\textup{U}_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}(\mathcal{U})\cong U$ in $\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$. It has the universal property that for any $\mathcal{Z}\in\mathcal{T}\textup{op}$ with underlying set $Z=\textup{U}_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}(\mathcal{Z})$, the square
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \hom_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U}) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{S}\textup{et}}(Z,U) \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \hom_{\mathcal{T}\textup{op}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y}) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{S}\textup{et}}(Z,Y) \end{array}$$
is a pullback square in $\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$. If $U\rightarrow Y$ is the inclusion of a subset, this specializes to the subspace topology on $U$.

## 2. Model-independent definitions

**Definition 2.1.** Let $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{B}$ be a functor of $\infty$-categories.

(1) A morphism $e_{1}\xrightarrow{\varphi}e_{2}$ in $\mathcal{E}$ is *$f$-cocartesian* (or simply *cocartesian*, if $f$ is clear from context) if it induces a pullback square in $\mathcal{C}\textup{at}_{\infty}$:
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \mathcal{E}_{e_{2}/} & \xrightarrow{-\circ\varphi} & \mathcal{E}_{e_{1}/} \\ \downarrow{\scriptstyle f} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle f} \\ \mathcal{B}_{f(e_{2})/} & \xrightarrow{-\circ f(\varphi)} & \mathcal{B}_{f(e_{1})/}. \end{array}$$
In this case, $\varphi$ is called an *$f$-cocartesian lift* (or simply a *cocartesian lift*) of $f(\varphi)$ relative to $e_{1}$. The functor $f$ is a *cocartesian fibration* if every object of $\textup{Fun}([1],\mathcal{B})\underset{s,\mathcal{B},f}{\times}\mathcal{E}$ admits an $f$-cocartesian lift.

(2) Dually, a morphism $e_{1}\xrightarrow{\varphi}e_{2}$ in $\mathcal{E}$ is *$f$-cartesian* (or simply *cartesian*) if it induces a pullback square in $\mathcal{C}\textup{at}_{\infty}$:
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \mathcal{E}_{/e_{1}} & \xrightarrow{\varphi\circ-} & \mathcal{E}_{/e_{2}} \\ \downarrow{\scriptstyle f} & & \downarrow{\scriptstyle f} \\ \mathcal{B}_{/f(e_{1})} & \xrightarrow{f(\varphi)\circ-} & \mathcal{B}_{/f(e_{2})}. \end{array}$$
In this case, $\varphi$ is called an *$f$-cartesian lift* (or simply a *cartesian lift*) of $f(\varphi)$ relative to $e_{2}$. The functor $f$ is a *cartesian fibration* if every object of $\textup{Fun}([1],\mathcal{B})\underset{t,\mathcal{B},f}{\times}\mathcal{E}$ admits an $f$-cartesian lift.

**Remark 2.2.** Let $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{B}$ be a functor of $\infty$-categories.

- If $e_{1}\xrightarrow{\varphi}e_{2}$ is $f$-cocartesian, then for every object $e\in\mathcal{E}$, the square
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \hom_{\mathcal{E}}(e_{2},e) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{E}}(e_{1},e) \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \hom_{\mathcal{B}}(f(e_{2}),f(e)) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{B}}(f(e_{1}),f(e)) \end{array}$$
is a pullback square in $\mathcal{S}$.

- If $e_{1}\xrightarrow{\varphi}e_{2}$ is $f$-cartesian, then for every object $e\in\mathcal{E}$, the square
$$\begin{array}{ccc} \hom_{\mathcal{E}}(e,e_{1}) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{E}}(e,e_{2}) \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \hom_{\mathcal{B}}(f(e),f(e_{1})) & \rightarrow & \hom_{\mathcal{B}}(f(e),f(e_{2})) \end{array}$$
is a pullback square in $\mathcal{S}$.

(Recall 1.6, and see [Lur09, Proposition 2.4.1.10].)

**Remark 2.3.** A cocartesian fibration $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{B}$ whose fibers $f^{-1}(b)$ are all $\infty$-groupoids is called a *left fibration*. These form the full subcategory $\mathcal{LF}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B})\subset\textup{co}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B})$. The covariant Grothendieck construction restricts to an equivalence
$$\textup{Fun}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\textup{at}_{\infty})\simeq\textup{co}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B}),\qquad \textup{Fun}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{S})\simeq\mathcal{LF}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B}),$$
where the vertical equivalences are given by the Grothendieck construction $\textup{Gr}$ from functors $\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}$ to left fibrations over $\mathcal{B}$. Since all morphisms in a space are equivalences, for a left fibration $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{B}$, every morphism in $\mathcal{E}$ is $f$-cocartesian.

Dually, a cartesian fibration whose fibers are all $\infty$-groupoids is a *right fibration*. The contravariant Grothendieck construction $\textup{Gr}^{-}$ restricts to an equivalence
$$\textup{Fun}(\mathcal{B}^{op},\mathcal{C}\textup{at}_{\infty})\simeq\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B}),\qquad \textup{Fun}(\mathcal{B}^{op},\mathcal{S})\simeq\mathcal{RF}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B}),$$
where $\mathcal{RF}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B})\subset\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}\textup{ib}(\mathcal{B})$ is the full subcategory of right fibrations over $\mathcal{B}$. Given a right fibration $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{B}$, every morphism in $\mathcal{E}$ is $f$-cartesian.

## 3. Quasicategorical comparison

**Notation 3.1.** Write $\underline{\hom}(-,-)=\underline{\hom}_{s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}}(-,-):(s\mathcal{S}\textup{et})^{op}\times s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}\rightarrow s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$ for the internal hom bifunctor in $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$, relative to its cartesian symmetric monoidal structure.

**Definition 3.2.** Let $\mathtt{E}\xrightarrow{\mathtt{p}}\mathtt{B}$ be an inner fibration in $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$. Following [Lur09, Definition 2.4.1.1], an edge $\Delta^{1}\xrightarrow{\mathtt{f}}\mathtt{E}$ is *JL-$\mathtt{p}$-cartesian* (or simply *JL-cartesian*) if the induced map
$$\mathtt{E}_{/\mathtt{f}}\rightarrow\mathtt{E}_{/\mathtt{f}(1)}\underset{\mathtt{B}_{/\mathtt{p}(\mathtt{f}(1))}}{\times}\mathtt{B}_{/\mathtt{p}(\mathtt{f})}$$
lies in $(\mathbf{W}\cap\mathbf{F})_{\textup{Joyal}}\subset s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}$.² In this case, the edge $\mathtt{f}\in\mathtt{E}_{1}$ is called a *JL-$\mathtt{p}$-cartesian lift* (or simply a *JL-cartesian lift*) of the edge $\mathtt{p}(\mathtt{f})\in\mathtt{B}_{1}$ relative to the vertex $\mathtt{f}(1)\in\mathtt{E}_{0}$.

Following [Lur09, Definition 2.4.2.1], $\mathtt{f}$ is a *JL-cartesian fibration* if every vertex of $\underline{\hom}(\Delta^{1},\mathtt{B})\underset{\textup{ev}_{1},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{f}}{\times}\mathtt{E}$ admits an $\mathtt{f}$-cartesian lift.

² The map $\mathtt{E}_{/\mathtt{f}}\rightarrow\mathtt{E}_{/\mathtt{f}(1)}$ is "postcomposition with $\mathtt{f}$". The restriction map $\mathtt{E}_{/\mathtt{f}}\rightarrow\mathtt{E}_{/\mathtt{f}(0)}$ lies in $(\mathbf{W}\cap\mathbf{F})_{\textup{Joyal}}$. The same holds for $\mathtt{B}_{/\mathtt{p}(\mathtt{f})}\rightarrow\mathtt{B}_{/\mathtt{p}(\mathtt{f}(1))}$.

**Theorem 3.3.** Let $\mathtt{E}\stackrel{\mathtt{p}}{\twoheadrightarrow}\mathtt{B}$ be a fibration between fibrant objects in $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{\textup{Joyal}}$ which presents a map $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{p}\mathcal{B}$ in $\mathcal{C}\textup{at}_{\infty}$. Suppose that a map $\Delta^{1}\xrightarrow{\mathtt{f}}\mathtt{E}$ in $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{\textup{Joyal}}$ presents a $p$-cartesian morphism $[1]\xrightarrow{f}\mathcal{E}$. Then the edge $\mathtt{f}\in\mathtt{E}_{1}$ is JL-$\mathtt{p}$-cartesian.

**Corollary 3.4.** Let $\mathtt{E}\stackrel{\mathtt{p}}{\twoheadrightarrow}\mathtt{B}$ be a fibration between fibrant objects in $s\mathcal{S}\textup{et}_{\textup{Joyal}}$ which presents a map $\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{p}\mathcal{B}$ in $\mathcal{C}\textup{at}_{\infty}$. If $p$ is a cartesian fibration, then $\mathtt{p}$ is a JL-cartesian fibration.
