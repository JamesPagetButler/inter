# Restatement

## Abstract

The lectures review Generalized Complex Geometry and discuss two applications to string theory: the description of supersymmetric flux compactifications, and the supersymmetric embedding of D-branes. The review covers G-structures, SU(3)-structure and its torsion classes, and then Generalized Complex Geometry. It treats the supersymmetry conditions of type II supergravity as differential conditions on pure spinors, and introduces generalized calibrations to describe D-branes. AdS₄ compactifications, which serve as geometric duals in the AdS₄/CFT₃ correspondence, are discussed in detail as examples.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation: Flux compactifications

Compactifications from ten dimensions (10D) to four dimensions (4D) are considered at the level of supergravity. The compactified solutions have the geometry of a product of four external dimensions and six compact internal dimensions. Supersymmetric solutions are considered for two reasons:

1. Supersymmetry conditions are simpler than the supergravity equations of motion. Solutions to these conditions, together with the Bianchi identities for the form fields, solve the full equations of motion.
2. Supersymmetry is expected to be broken at a scale much lower than the compactification scale.

Without form fluxes, the supersymmetric internal six-dimensional (6D) manifolds are exactly the Calabi–Yau manifolds (including tori). Such compactifications contain massless scalar fields (moduli) in the 4D effective theory. These describe the shape and size of the Calabi–Yau, or the values of form potentials integrated over cycles. Moduli can be lifted by turning on expectation values of form field strengths (fluxes)¹, which complicates the supersymmetry conditions.

- In type IIB with D3/D7-branes and O3-planes, the geometry is a conformal Calabi–Yau manifold (a Calabi–Yau up to a rescaling of the metric). Seminal work is cited in [6, 7].
- F-theory on eight-dimensional Calabi–Yau manifolds is a related setup.

Generically, solutions with fluxes lie far from the Calabi–Yau limit. Generalized Complex Geometry, pioneered in [8, 9], unifies and generalizes complex and symplectic geometry. It describes supersymmetric compactifications with fluxes [10] and the supersymmetric embedding of D-branes with world-volume gauge flux.

Generalized Complex Geometry also applies to the world-sheet theory of strings in curved backgrounds with NSNS three-form flux [11–15, bihermitian geometry of 16], to supersymmetric D-branes in that setup [17, 18], and to a topological world-sheet model with three-form flux [19–22]. The RNS world-sheet approach cannot include RR fields; these are covered in the review [23].

**AdS/CFT.** The construction of AdS compactifications with fluxes is easier than Minkowski or de Sitter compactifications, because the no-go theorem of Maldacena–Núñez [24] (theorem 4.2) does not apply. Localized orientifold planes are therefore not needed. Examples in AdS₄/CFT₃ appear in section 4.3. Work in AdS₅/CFT₄ is in [25–27].

**Structure of the review.** Chapter 2 reviews complex geometry, G-structures, torsion classes and Calabi–Yau geometry. Chapter 3 introduces Generalized Complex Geometry. Chapter 4 treats background supersymmetry conditions with fluxes, and chapter 5 treats D-brane supersymmetry conditions. Each chapter ends with exercises, graded from "easy" to "hard", with literature references for intermediate and hard exercises.

### 1.2 Invitation: supersymmetry conditions in the fluxless case

Type II supergravity contains a metric, a dilaton, and form fields from the NSNS and RR sectors. Setting the form fluxes to zero, a state with some unbroken supersymmetry solves the equations of motion. For fluxes, the exact statement is theorem 4.1 in chapter 4. See [28, Chapter 15] for a detailed review.

Compactify on a 4D Minkowski space times an internal space with curved metric. The supersymmetry variations of the gravitini and dilatini are, in the absence of fluxes,

$$
\delta\psi^{1,2}_{M}=\nabla_{M}\epsilon^{1,2}, \tag{1.1}
$$

$$
\delta\lambda^{1,2}=\underline{\partial}\Phi\,\epsilon_{1,2}. \tag{1.2}
$$

Supersymmetry requires existence of $\epsilon^{1,2}$ with $\delta\psi^{1,2}_M=\delta\lambda^{1,2}=0$. The number of such $\epsilon^{1,2}$ gives the number of supercharges.

These conditions have topological and differential parts.

**Topological part.** The structure group reduces from Spin(9,1) to Spin(3,1) × Spin(6). A 4D Poincaré-invariant spinor vev is forbidden, so the fermion vevs vanish. The internal spinor $\eta$ (equal for $\epsilon^1$ and $\epsilon^2$) must be globally defined and non-vanishing. For a positive-chirality Weyl spinor $\eta_+$, the stabilizer in Spin(6) ≅ SU(4) is SU(3). So the internal structure group reduces from Spin(6) to SU(3).

**Differential part.** $\delta\psi_M=0$ makes $\eta_+$ covariantly constant. The internal space then has SU(3) holonomy, and is a Calabi–Yau manifold. The holonomy depends on the metric, while the structure group does not. $\delta\lambda=0$ makes the dilaton constant.

With constant 4D spinors and the 10D Majorana condition, this gives eight real supercharges, i.e. $\mathcal{N}=2$ supersymmetry in 4D. RR fluxes relate $\epsilon^1$ and $\epsilon^2$, so the 4D parts cannot be chosen independently; the configuration then has $\mathcal{N}=1$ supersymmetry.

**Alternative description of SU(3)-structure.** A nowhere-vanishing spinor $\eta_+$ defines a real two-form $\omega$ and a complex three-form $\Omega$:

$$
|\eta_+|^2\,\omega_{ij}=i\,\eta_+^\dagger\gamma_{ij}\eta_+,\qquad |\eta_+|^2\,\Omega_{i_1i_2i_3}=\eta_-^\dagger\gamma_{i_1i_2i_3}\eta_+, \tag{1.5}
$$

where $\eta_-$ is the complex conjugate of $\eta_+$ and $|\eta_+|^2=\eta_+^\dagger\eta_+$. They satisfy

$$
\omega\wedge\Omega=0,\qquad \Omega\wedge\bar\Omega=\frac{8i}{3!}\omega^3=8i\,\mathrm{vol}_6. \tag{1.6}
$$

$\Omega$ is decomposable (a wedge product of three one-forms). Conversely, a real two-form and a decomposable complex three-form satisfying (1.6) define a metric; if the metric is positive-definite, they define an SU(3)-structure. SU(3)-holonomy is then

$$
d\omega=d\Omega=0. \tag{1.7}
$$

$\omega$ and $\Omega$ are the Kähler form and the holomorphic (3,0)-form.

**D-branes and calibrations.** Supersymmetric D-branes without world-volume gauge flux in a Calabi–Yau background wrap complex or special Lagrangian submanifolds. These are calibrated with respect to

$$
\frac{1}{l!}\omega^{l},\qquad \mathrm{Re}\left(e^{i\alpha}\Omega\right), \tag{1.8}
$$

where $0\le l\le 3$ and $e^{i\alpha}$ is a constant phase. The lectures extend this to NSNS and RR fluxes and to world-volume fluxes. For the most general case, where $\epsilon^{1,2}$ involve different internal spinors $\eta^{(1,2)}$, the framework is Generalized Complex Geometry. It treats complex geometry (based on $\Omega$) and symplectic geometry (based on $\omega$) on the same footing.

## 2 Chapter 2: G-structures and complex geometry

**Definition 2.1.** The tangent frame bundle $FM$, associated to the tangent bundle $TM$, is the bundle over $M$ whose fiber at each $p\in M$ is the set of ordered bases of $T_pM$.

**Definition 2.2.** A manifold $M$ has a $G$-structure, with $G\subset GL(d,\mathbb{R})$, if the tangent frame bundle can be reduced to have structure group $G$.

**Example 2.1 (Metric and orientation).** If there is a globally defined, positive-definite symmetric two-tensor $g\in\Gamma(S^2(T^*M))$, then $g$ is non-degenerate and the structure group reduces to $O(d,\mathbb{R})$. Then $g$ is a metric and $M$ is Riemannian. If there is also a globally defined volume form $\mathrm{vol}_d$ associated to the metric, $M$ is orientable and the structure group reduces to $SO(d,\mathbb{R})$.

**Example 2.2 (Almost complex structure).** Let $J\in\Gamma(TM\otimes T^*M)$, equivalently a bundle map $J:TM\to TM$ with $\pi(Jv)=\pi(v)$, satisfying

$$
J^2=-\mathbb{1}. \tag{2.8}
$$

This is non-degenerate, and the structure group reduces to $GL(d/2,\mathbb{C})$. $J$ is an almost complex structure. Such a $J$ exists only if $d$ is even. The eigenvalues of $J$ are $\pm i$. Working in the complexification $TM\otimes\mathbb{C}$, the $(+i)$- and $(-i)$-eigenbundles are $L,\bar L\subset TM\otimes\mathbb{C}$. Both have rank $d/2$ (equal rank, since $J$ is real). Subbundles locally spanned by smooth vector fields are called distributions.

**Example 2.3 (Pre-symplectic structure).** If $\omega\in\Gamma(\Lambda^2T^*M)$ is a globally defined non-degenerate two-form, the structure group reduces to $\mathrm{Sp}(d,\mathbb{R})$. $\omega$ is non-degenerate if and only if $\omega^{d/2}\neq 0$.

**Example 2.4 (Hermitian metric).** A metric $g$ and an almost complex structure $J$ satisfying

$$
J^{i}{}_{k}\,g_{ij}\,J^{j}{}_{l}=g_{kl} \tag{2.9-hermiticity}
$$

reduce the structure group to $U(d/2)$. Hermiticity makes $\omega_{ij}=g_{ik}J^k{}_j$ antisymmetric, so a pre-symplectic structure is automatically present. Conversely, $J$ and $\omega$ satisfying

$$
J^{i}{}_{k}\,\omega_{ij}\,J^{j}{}_{l}=\omega_{kl} \tag{2.12}
$$

define a metric $g_{ij}=-\omega_{ik}J^k{}_j$. A pre-symplectic structure and a metric also define an almost complex structure. Any two of the three structures imply the third.

**Example 2.5 (Almost product structure).** Let $R\in\Gamma(TM\otimes T^*M)$ satisfy

$$
R^2=\mathbb{1}. \tag{2.14}
$$

The $(+1)$- and $(-1)$-eigenbundles are $T,N\subset TM$. If the $(+1)$-eigenspace has dimension $l$, the structure group reduces to $GL(l,\mathbb{R})\times GL(d-l,\mathbb{R})$, with ranks $l$ and $d-l$ (generically unequal). If additionally $R$ is orthogonal with respect to a metric $g$,

$$
R^{i}{}_{k}\,g_{ij}\,R^{j}{}_{l}=g_{kl}, \tag{2.16}
$$

the structure group reduces to $O(l)\times O(d-l)$.

**Definition 2.3.** The Lie bracket of vector fields $X,Y$ is the vector field $[X,Y]$ defined by

$$
\mathcal{L}_{[X,Y]}f=\mathcal{L}_X\mathcal{L}_Yf-\mathcal{L}_Y\mathcal{L}_Xf \tag{2.18}
$$

for every function $f$.

**Definition 2.4.** A distribution $L$ is involutive if, for all $X,Y\in\Gamma(L)$, $[X,Y]\in\Gamma(L)$. (2.20)

**Definition 2.5.** A distribution $L$ is integrable if through every point $p\in M$ there exists a solution to eq. (2.21) in a neighbourhood of $p$.

**Theorem 2.1 (Frobenius).** $L$ is integrable if and only if it is involutive.

**Example 2.6 (Complex structure).** If the $(+i)$-eigenbundle $L$ of an almost complex structure $J$ is integrable, adapted coordinates $z^a$ exist with $L$ spanned by $\{\partial/\partial z^a\}$. These are holomorphic coordinates, and $J$ is a complex structure. The transition functions are restricted to $GL(d/2,\mathbb{C})$, so coordinate changes between patches are holomorphic. Integrability of $L$ is equivalent to integrability of $\bar L$. By the Frobenius theorem, integrability is equivalent to

$$
JX=iX\ \text{and}\ JY=iY\ \Rightarrow\ J[X,Y]=i[X,Y]. \tag{2.22}
$$

The Nijenhuis tensor is

$$
N_J(X,Y)=J[JX,Y]+J[X,JY]-[JX,JY]-J^2[X,Y]=J[JX,Y]+J[X,JY]-[JX,JY]+[X,Y], \tag{2.23}
$$

and $J$ is integrable if and only if

$$
N_J(X,Y)=0 \tag{2.24}
$$

for all $X,Y$.

**Example 2.7 (Product structure).** For an almost product structure $R$,

$$
N_R(X,Y)=R[RX,Y]+R[X,RY]-[RX,RY]-[X,Y]. \tag{2.25}
$$

Integrability of $T$ and of $N$ are independent:

$$
T\ \text{integrable}\iff N_R(X,Y)|_N=0,\qquad N\ \text{integrable}\iff N_R(X,Y)|_T=0, \tag{2.26}
$$

where $|_N$ and $|_T$ denote projections. If $T$ is integrable of rank $l$, it defines $l$-dimensional submanifolds (leaves of a foliation) through each point. When both $T$ and $N$ are integrable, $R$ is a product structure, and the manifold can be foliated in two ways (figure 2). Only integrability of $T$ is needed in chapter 5, where $T$ is defined on one submanifold, which corresponds to a D-brane in Generalized Complex Geometry.

**Example 2.8 (Symplectic structure).** A pre-symplectic $\omega$ is integrable if

$$
d\omega=0, \tag{2.27}
$$

and is then a symplectic structure. For integrable $\omega$ there are Darboux coordinates $(q^a,p_a)$ with

$$
\omega=\sum_a dp_a\wedge dq^a. \tag{2.28}
$$

Chapter 3 treats symplectic structures on the same footing as complex structures, via subbundles of the generalized tangent bundle.

**Example 2.9 (Almost complex structure, forms).** $TM\otimes\mathbb{C}=L\oplus\bar L$ induces $T^*M\otimes\mathbb{C}=\Lambda^{1,0}T^*M\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}T^*M$, each of rank $d/2$. Here $\theta\in\Lambda^{1,0}$ iff $\theta(X)=0$ for all $X\in\bar L$, and similarly for $\Lambda^{0,1}$. Higher forms decompose as

$$
\Lambda^lT^*M=\bigoplus_{0\le p\le l}\Lambda^{p,l-p}T^*M, \tag{2.31}
$$

with $\Omega^{p,q}(M)=\Gamma(\Lambda^{p,q}T^*M)$. A local frame of $d/2$ independent $(1,0)$-forms $\theta^a$ defines a local section of the canonical bundle $\Lambda^{d/2,0}T^*M$:

$$
\Omega=\theta^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta^{d/2}. \tag{2.33}
$$

Conversely, $\bar L=\{v\in TM\mid \iota_v\Omega=0\}$ (2.34), which determines $L$ and $J$. $J$ determines the $\theta^a$ only up to $GL(d/2,\mathbb{C})$, so $\Omega$ is determined up to an overall complex function. Requiring $\Omega$ to be globally defined and nowhere vanishing reduces the structure group to $SL(d/2,\mathbb{C})$. $\Omega$ must be decomposable (locally a wedge product of one-forms).

For $d=6$, reference [33] gives a way to construct a decomposable three-form from a real stable three-form $\rho$. Define

$$
\tilde J^{\,i}{}_{j}=\varepsilon^{ii_1\dots i_5}\rho_{ji_1i_2}\rho_{i_3i_4i_5}, \tag{2.35}
$$

with $\varepsilon^{i_1\dots i_d}=\pm1$ the totally antisymmetric symbol, and

$$
q(\rho)=\frac{1}{6}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\tilde J^2. \tag{2.36}
$$

$\rho$ is stable if $q(\rho)\neq0$. For $q(\rho)<0$,

$$
J=\pm\tilde J/\sqrt{-q(\rho)}, \tag{2.37}
$$

$$
\mathrm{Re}\,\Omega=\rho,\qquad \mathrm{Im}\,\Omega=\hat\rho=\tfrac16 J^{i}{}_{j}\left(\iota_i\wedge dx^j-dx^j\wedge\iota_i\right)\rho. \tag{2.38}
$$

The Hitchin function is $H(\rho)=\sqrt{-q(\rho)}$. $J$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,\Omega$ are determined (up to sign) by $\mathrm{Re}\,\Omega=\rho$. In other dimensions, $J$ can be determined from $\mathrm{Re}\,\Omega$ via

$$
J^{i}{}_{j}=c\,\varepsilon^{ii_1\dots i_{d-1}}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Omega_{ji_1\dots i_{d/2-1}}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Omega_{i_{d/2}\dots i_{d-1}}, \tag{2.39}
$$

with $c$ a function fixed by $J^2=-\mathbb{1}$. Not every real form with negative $q(\rho)$ yields a proper almost complex structure this way.

**Example 2.10 (Hermitian pre-symplectic structure, or $U(d/2)$-structure).** For $\omega\in\Omega^2(M,\mathbb{R})$ satisfying the hermiticity condition (2.12), the condition is equivalent to $\omega\in\Omega^{1,1}(M)$. For real $\omega$, this is

$$
\omega\wedge\Omega=0. \tag{2.41}
$$

Section 3.3 argues that the appropriate associated polyform in Generalized Complex Geometry is $e^{i\omega}$.

**Example 2.11 ($SU(d/2)$-structure).** An almost complex structure with a compatible pre-symplectic structure reduces the structure group to $U(d/2)$. A globally defined, decomposable $(d/2,0)$-form $\Omega$ that is non-degenerate everywhere, $\Omega\wedge\bar\Omega\neq0$ (2.42), fixes the overall factor and reduces the structure group further to $SU(d/2)$, provided $\omega$ is non-degenerate, satisfies (2.41), and the associated metric is positive-definite. The normalization is usually

$$
\Omega\wedge\bar\Omega=(-1)^{\frac{d/2(d/2-1)}{2}}\,\frac{(2i\omega)^{d/2}}{(d/2)!}. \tag{2.43}
$$

Then there is a local basis $\theta^a$ of $(1,0)$-forms with

$$
J=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_a\theta^a\wedge\bar\theta^{\bar a},\qquad \Omega=\theta^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta^{d/2}. \tag{2.44}
$$

**Example 2.12 (Almost product structure).** Let $T$ have rank $l$. The annihilator is

$$
\mathrm{Ann}\,T=\{\theta\in T^*M\mid \theta(v)=0\ \forall v\in T\}. \tag{2.45}
$$

A local frame of $d-l$ independent real one-forms $\theta^a$ spanning $\mathrm{Ann}\,T$ defines a real section

$$
\tau=\theta^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta^{d-l}, \tag{2.46}
$$

and $T=\{v\in TM\mid \iota_v\tau=0\}$ (2.47). Under $GL(l,\mathbb{R})\times GL(d-l,\mathbb{R})$, $\tau$ is defined up to an overall factor.

**Definition 2.6 (Interior product).** For $X\in\Gamma(T^*M)$ and $\phi\in\Omega^l(M)$, $\iota_X\phi$ is the $(l-1)$-form with

$$
\iota_X\phi(Y_1,\dots,Y_{l-1})=\phi(X,Y_1,\dots,Y_{l-1}) \tag{2.48}
$$

for all $Y_i\in\Gamma(TM)$.

**Definition 2.7 (Exterior derivative).** For $\phi\in\Omega^l(M)$, $d\phi\in\Omega^{l+1}(M)$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\phi(Y_0,\dots,Y_l)={}&\sum_{0\le a\le l}(-1)^aY_a\big(\phi(Y_0,\dots,\hat Y_a,\dots,Y_l)\big)\\
&+\sum_{0\le a<b\le l}(-1)^{a+b}\phi([Y_a,Y_b],Y_0,\dots,\hat Y_a,\dots,\hat Y_b,\dots,Y_l),
\end{aligned} \tag{2.51}
$$

for all $Y_0,\dots,Y_l\in\Gamma(TM)$, where $\hat Y_a$ means that $Y_a$ is omitted.

**Definition 2.8 (Lie derivative).** $\mathcal{L}_X:\Omega^l(M)\to\Omega^l(M)$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}_X=\{\iota_X,d\}=\iota_Xd+d\,\iota_X. \tag{2.53}
$$

**Theorem 2.2 (Calabi–Yau).** Let $M$ be a compact Kähler manifold of dimension $d$, with Kähler form $\tilde\omega$ and complex structure $J$, admitting a globally defined nowhere-vanishing $(d/2,0)$-form $\Omega$. Then there is a unique metric with Kähler form $\omega$ in the same Kähler class as $\tilde\omega$, meaning $\tilde\omega=\omega+d\alpha$, such that $(f\Omega,\omega)$ is Calabi–Yau, where $f$ is a suitable normalization function.

**Example 2.13 (Complex deformations).** Consider an infinitesimal deformation of an integrable complex structure $J$,

$$
J'^{\,i}{}_k=J^i{}_k+\tau^i{}_k. \tag{2.82}
$$

The new structure must satisfy (2.8), which gives

$$
\tau^i{}_jJ^j{}_k+J^i{}_j\tau^j{}_k=0. \tag{2.83}
$$

In holomorphic coordinates this reads $\tau^a{}_b=0$ and $\tau^{\bar a}{}_{\bar b}=0$ (2.84), so only mixed components can be non-zero. Vanishing of the Nijenhuis tensor of $J'$ gives

$$
\partial_{[\bar a}\tau^c{}_{\bar b]}=0. \tag{2.85}
$$

A deformation $\tau^a{}_{\bar b}=\epsilon\,\bar\partial_{\bar b}v^a$ (2.86) is trivial, since it is the coordinate change $z^a\to z^a+i\epsilon v^a(z,\bar z)$.
