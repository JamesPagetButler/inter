# Restatement

## Abstract

The paper constructs the propagator of the massless Dirac operator $W$ on a closed Riemannian 3-manifold as the sum of two invariantly defined oscillatory integrals, global in space and in time, with distinguished complex-valued phase functions. The two oscillatory integrals, the positive and the negative propagators, correspond to the positive and negative eigenvalues of $W$, respectively. The construction yields a global invariant definition of the full symbols of the propagators (scalar matrix-functions on the cotangent bundle), a closed formula for the principal symbols, and an algorithm for computing all their homogeneous components. It also gives small-time expansions of the principal and subprincipal symbols in terms of geometric invariants, and the third local Weyl coefficients in the asymptotic expansion of the eigenvalue counting functions of $W$.

## 1 Statement of the problem

Let $(M,g)$ be a connected, oriented, closed Riemannian 3-manifold. Denote by $\nabla$ the Levi-Civita connection, by $\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma}$ the Christoffel symbols, and by

$$\rho(x):=\sqrt{\det g_{\alpha\beta}(x)} \tag{1.1}$$

the Riemannian density.

Let $e_j$, $j=1,2,3$, be a positively oriented global framing, i.e. three orthonormal smooth vector fields whose orientation agrees with that of $M$. (An orientable 3-manifold is automatically parallelizable [37, 48].) In local coordinates $x^\alpha$, $\alpha=1,2,3$, $e_j{}^\alpha$ denotes the $\alpha$-th component of the $j$-th vector field. Greek indices are holonomic and Latin indices are anholonomic. Repeated indices are summed.

Let

$$s^1:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=s_1,\quad s^2:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}=s_2,\quad s^3:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=s_3 \tag{1.2}$$

be the standard Pauli matrices, and let

$$\sigma^\alpha:=s^j\,e_j{}^\alpha \tag{1.3}$$

be their projection along the framing. $\sigma^\alpha$ is a vector-valued function with values in trace-free Hermitian $2\times 2$ matrices.

**Definition 1.1.** We call *massless Dirac operator* the operator

$$W:=-i\sigma^{\alpha}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{4}\sigma_{\beta}\left(\frac{\partial\sigma^{\beta}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}+\Gamma^{\beta}{}_{\alpha\gamma}\,\sigma^{\gamma}\right)\right):H^{1}(M;\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C}^{2}). \tag{1.4}$$

Here $H^1$ is the usual Sobolev space of functions that are square integrable together with their first partial derivatives.

**Remark 1.2.** The Dirac operator admits several equivalent definitions. The most common is the geometric definition in terms of spinor bundles. The analytic Definition 1.1 is equivalent to the standard geometric one, see [28, Appendix B].

Definition 1.1 depends on the choice of framing. Let

$$G:M\to \mathrm{SU}(2) \tag{1.5}$$

be a smooth special unitary matrix-function, and let $\widetilde W$ be the Dirac operator for a given framing. The transformation

$$\widetilde{W}\mapsto G^{*}\widetilde{W}G:=W, \tag{1.6}$$

where $*$ denotes Hermitian conjugation, produces a Dirac operator for a different framing. Conversely, given two Dirac operators $\widetilde W$ and $W$, a smooth $G$ as in (1.5) with $W=G^*\widetilde W G$ exists under a closeness condition, but in general topological obstructions prevent it. This motivates the notion of spin structure, see [6, 19]. The gauge transformation (1.5), (1.6) is the operator-level manifestation of pointwise rotation of the framing,

$$\widetilde{e}_{j}\mapsto O_{j}{}^{k}\,\widetilde{e}_{k}=:e_{j},\qquad O\in C^{\infty}(M;\mathrm{SO}(3)), \tag{1.7}$$

via the double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2)\xrightarrow{2:1}\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The Dirac operator (1.4) is uniquely determined by the metric and spin structure modulo an $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ gauge transformation.

The Dirac operator is symmetric with respect to the $L^2$ inner product

$$\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{M}u^{*}v\,\rho\,dx,\qquad u,v\in L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C}^{2}), \tag{1.8}$$

where $dx=dx^1dx^2dx^3$. It is elliptic, meaning that the determinant of the principal symbol does not vanish on $T^*M\setminus\{0\}$. The Dirac operator is self-adjoint with discrete spectrum accumulating at $+\infty$ and at $-\infty$. Let $\lambda_k$ be the eigenvalues of $W$, counted with multiplicity, and $v_k$ the corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

The Dirac operator commutes with the antilinear charge conjugation

$$v=\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\v_2\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{v_2}\\\overline{v_1}\end{pmatrix}=:\mathrm{C}(v),$$

see [24, Appendix A]. This implies that all eigenvalues have even multiplicity.

**Definition 1.3.** We define the *Dirac propagator* as

$$U(t):=e^{-itW}. \tag{1.9}$$

It is the (distributional) solution of the Cauchy problem

$$\left(-i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+W\right)U=0, \tag{1.10a}$$
$$U(0)=\operatorname{Id}. \tag{1.10b}$$

It is a time-dependent unitary operator, given by functional calculus as

$$U(t)=\sum_{\lambda_k}e^{-it\lambda_k}\,v_k\,\langle v_k,\cdot\,\rangle. \tag{1.11}$$

The Dirac propagator splits as $U(t)=U^{+}(t)+U^{0}+U^{-}(t)$, where

$$U^{+}(t):=\sum_{\lambda_k>0}e^{-it\lambda_k}\,v_k\,\langle v_k,\cdot\,\rangle, \tag{1.12a}$$
$$U^{0}:=\sum_{\lambda_k=0}v_k\,\langle v_k,\cdot\,\rangle, \tag{1.12b}$$
$$U^{-}(t):=\sum_{\lambda_k<0}e^{-it\lambda_k}\,v_k\,\langle v_k,\cdot\,\rangle. \tag{1.12c}$$

The operators (1.12a), (1.12b), (1.12c) are called the *positive*, *zero mode*, and *negative propagators*, respectively. They are time-dependent partial isometries. $U^0$ is nontrivial only if $0$ is an eigenvalue of $W$.

The positive ($+$) and negative ($-$) local counting functions are

$$N_{\pm}(y;\lambda):=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }\lambda\leq 0,\\\sum_{0<\pm\lambda_k<\lambda}[v_k(y)]^{*}\,v_k(y)&\text{for }\lambda>0.\end{cases} \tag{1.13}$$

Integrating over $M$ gives

$$N_{\pm}(\lambda):=\int_{M}N_{\pm}(y;\lambda)\,\rho(y)\,dy=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }\lambda\leq 0,\\\sum_{0<\pm\lambda_k<\lambda}1&\text{for }\lambda>0.\end{cases} \tag{1.14}$$

Let $\hat\mu:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}$ be a smooth function with $\hat\mu=1$ in a neighbourhood of the origin and $\operatorname{supp}\hat\mu\subset(-T_0,T_0)$, where $T_0$ is the infimum of the lengths of all geodesic loops originating from all points of $M$. The Fourier transform is

$$\mathcal{F}_{\lambda\to t}[f](t)=\hat f(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-it\lambda}f(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \tag{1.15}$$

and the inverse Fourier transform is

$$\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{t\to\lambda}[\hat f](\lambda)=f(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{it\lambda}\hat f(t)\,dt. \tag{1.16}$$

Set $\mu:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\hat\mu]$. The mollified derivative of the positive (resp. negative) counting function has a complete asymptotic expansion in integer powers of $\lambda$ [26, 34, 35, 36, 45]:

$$(N_{\pm}^{\prime}*\mu)(y,\lambda)=c^{\pm}_{2}(y)\,\lambda^{2}+c^{\pm}_{1}(y)\,\lambda+c^{\pm}_{0}(y)+\dots\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to+\infty. \tag{1.17}$$

Here $*$ denotes convolution in $\lambda$.

**Definition 1.4.** We call *local Weyl coefficients* the smooth functions $c^{\pm}_{k}(y)$ appearing in the asymptotic expansions (1.17).

**Remark 1.5.**

(i) The definition of the Weyl coefficients does not depend on the choice of mollifier $\mu$. If $\widetilde\mu$ is another mollifier with the same support properties, then
$$(N_{\pm}^{\prime}*\mu)(y,\lambda)-(N_{\pm}^{\prime}*\widetilde{\mu})(y,\lambda)=O(\lambda^{-\infty})\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to+\infty.$$

(ii) The convention used here differs from the standard one in the literature, which calls the coefficients in the expansion of the mollified counting function $N*\mu$ the local Weyl coefficients. The two conventions differ by integrating factors:
$$(N_{\pm}*\mu)(y,\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}(N_{\pm}^{\prime}*\mu)(y,\kappa)\,d\kappa =\frac{1}{3}\,c^{\pm}_{2}(y)\,\lambda^{3}+\frac{1}{2}\,c^{\pm}_{1}(y)\,\lambda^{2}+c^{\pm}_{0}(y)\,\lambda+\dots\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to+\infty. \tag{1.18}$$
The paper uses Definition 1.4 throughout.

(iii) It was shown in [24] that
$$c^{\pm}_{2}(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}},\qquad c^{\pm}_{1}(y)=0. \tag{1.19}$$

(iv) The unmollified counting functions $N_{\pm}(y,\lambda)$ also admit asymptotic expansions as $\lambda\to+\infty$, but these are more delicate because the functions are discontinuous and number-theoretic issues arise. It is known [23, 24] that
$$N_{\pm}(y,\lambda)=\frac{1}{6\pi^{2}}\,\lambda^{3}+O(\lambda^{2})\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to+\infty$$
uniformly over $y\in M$, and that, under appropriate assumptions on geodesic loops,
$$N_{\pm}(y,\lambda)=\frac{1}{6\pi^{2}}\,\lambda^{3}+o(\lambda^{2})\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to+\infty.$$

(v) Spectral asymmetry [1, 2, 3, 4] is an important topic for first-order elliptic systems. Observing spectral asymmetry for the Dirac operator requires going as far as the sixth Weyl coefficients. This follows from the fact [12, 31] that the eta function
$$\eta(s):=\sum_{\lambda_k\neq 0}\frac{\operatorname{sgn}\lambda_k}{|\lambda_k|^{s}}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{-s}\big(N^{\prime}_{+}(\lambda)-N^{\prime}_{-}(\lambda)\big)\,d\lambda$$
is holomorphic in $\operatorname{Re}s>-2$ and has its first pole at $s=-2$. The residue of the eta function at $s=-2$, computed explicitly by Branson and Gilkey [14], equals
$$\int_{M}\big(c^{+}_{-3}(y)-c^{-}_{-3}(y)\big)\,\rho(y)\,dy,$$
which is the difference between the sixth (global) Weyl coefficients.

The paper has two objectives:

- **Objective 1.** Construct the propagators $U^{\pm}(t)$ explicitly, modulo integral operators with infinitely smooth kernels, as a single invariantly defined oscillatory integral, global in space and in time.
- **Objective 2.** Compute the third Weyl coefficient $c^{\pm}_{0}(y)$.

**Remark 1.6.** In general, the third Weyl coefficient cannot be identified from the asymptotic behaviour of the unmollified counting function. For the 3-torus with the standard flat metric, the statement
$$N_{\pm}(y,\lambda)=\frac{1}{6\pi^{2}}\,\lambda^{3}+c^{\pm}_{0}(y)\,\lambda+o(\lambda)\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to+\infty$$
is false. This follows by writing the eigenvalues explicitly as in [24, Appendix B] and using standard results from analytic number theory [32].

## 3 Oscillatory integrals and propagators

**Notation 3.1.** Let $v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times M_x\times M_y)$, $(\lambda,x,y)\mapsto v(\lambda,x,y)$. Write $v=O(|\lambda|^{-\infty})$ as $\lambda\to\pm\infty$ if for every $\alpha>0$, every $k\in\mathbb{N}$, and every linear partial differential operator $P$ of order $k$ with infinitely smooth coefficients on $M_x\times M_y$, there exists a constant $C_{\alpha,P}>0$ such that
$$|Pv|\leq C_{\alpha,P}\,|\lambda|^{-\alpha}\quad\text{for}\quad\pm\lambda>1,$$
uniformly over $M_x\times M_y$.

**Theorem 3.2.** Let $(T_-,T_+)\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be an open interval (possibly the whole real line). Let $u^{+}(t,x,y)$, $u^{-}(t,x,y)$, $\widetilde u^{+}(t,x,y)$ and $\widetilde u^{-}(t,x,y)$ be elements of $C^{\infty}(M_x\times M_y;\mathcal{D}'(T_-,T_+))$ satisfying:

(a) $u^{+}(t,x,y)+u^{-}(t,x,y)=\widetilde u^{+}(t,x,y)+\widetilde u^{-}(t,x,y)\mod C^{\infty}((T_-,T_+)\times M_x\times M_y)$.

Assume that for every $\zeta\in C^{\infty}_0(T_-,T_+)$:

(b) $\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{t\to\lambda}[\zeta\,u^{\pm}]=O(|\lambda|^{-\infty})$ as $\lambda\to\mp\infty$,

(c) $\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{t\to\lambda}[\zeta\,\widetilde u^{\pm}]=O(|\lambda|^{-\infty})$ as $\lambda\to\mp\infty$.

Then
$$u^{\pm}(t,x,y)=\widetilde u^{\pm}(t,x,y)\mod C^{\infty}((T_-,T_+)\times M_x\times M_y). \tag{3.4}$$

**Theorem 3.3.** Let $A$ be an elliptic self-adjoint first-order pseudodifferential operator acting on $m$-columns of scalar functions over $M$, whose principal symbol has simple eigenvalues. The positive and negative propagators $U^{+}_{A}(t)$ and $U^{-}_{A}(t)$ can be written, modulo an infinitely smoothing operator, as finite sums of oscillatory integrals, global in space and in time. More precisely,

$$U^{+}_{A}(t)\overset{\mod\Psi^{-\infty}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{+}}U^{(j)}_{A}(t), \tag{3.8}$$
$$U^{-}_{A}(t)\overset{\mod\Psi^{-\infty}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{-}}U^{(-j)}_{A}(t), \tag{3.9}$$

where

$$U^{(j)}_{A}(t):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{T'M}e^{i\varphi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)}\,\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}(t;y,\eta)\,\chi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)\,w^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)\,(\,\cdot\,)\,\rho(y)\,dy\,d\eta. \tag{3.10}$$

The notation is as follows.

- $\overset{\mod\Psi^{-\infty}}{=}$ means that the operator on the left equals the operator on the right up to an integral operator with infinitely smooth kernel.
- $(\cdot)$ denotes insertion of $f_0(y)$ when computing $(U^{(j)}_A f_0)(x)$.
- The phase function $\varphi^{(j)}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times M\times T'M;\mathbb{C})$ satisfies:
  - (i) $\varphi^{(j)}|_{x=x^{(j)}}=0$;
  - (ii) $\varphi^{(j)}_{x^\alpha}|_{x=x^{(j)}}=\xi^{(j)}_\alpha$;
  - (iii) $\det\varphi^{(j)}_{x^\alpha\eta_\beta}|_{x=x^{(j)}}\neq 0$;
  - (iv) $\operatorname{Im}\varphi^{(j)}\geq 0$.
- The symbol $\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}\in S^0_{\mathrm{ph}}(\mathbb{R}\times T'M;\mathrm{Mat}(m;\mathbb{C}))$ is a polyhomogeneous symbol of order zero with values in $m\times m$ complex matrices. That is, it has an asymptotic expansion in components positively homogeneous in momentum:
$$\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}(t;y,\eta)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}_{-k}(t;y,\eta),\qquad \mathfrak{a}^{(j)}_{-k}(t;y,\alpha\eta)=\alpha^{-k}\,\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}_{-k}(t;y,\eta),\quad\forall\alpha>0. \tag{3.11}$$
- The cut-off $\chi^{(j)}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times M\times T'M)$ satisfies:
  - (I) $\chi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)=0$ on $\{(t,x,y,\eta)\,|\,|h^{(j)}(y,\eta)|\leq 1/2\}$;
  - (II) $\chi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)=1$ on the intersection of $\{(t,x,y,\eta)\,|\,|h^{(j)}(y,\eta)|\geq 1\}$ with some conical neighbourhood of $\{(t,x^{(j)}(t;y,\eta),y,\eta)\}$;
  - (III) $\chi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\alpha\eta)=\chi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)$ for $\alpha\geq 1$ on $\{(t,x,y,\eta)\,|\,|h^{(j)}(y,\eta)|\geq 1\}$.
- The weight is
$$w^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta):=[\rho(x)\,\rho(y)]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\det^{2}(\varphi^{(j)}_{x^\alpha\eta_\beta})\right]^{\frac14}, \tag{3.12}$$
where the smooth branch of the complex root is chosen so that $w^{(j)}(0,y;y,\eta)=[\rho(y)]^{-1}$.

**Remark 3.4.** The weight $w^{(j)}$ is the inverse of a smooth density in $y$ and a smooth scalar function in all other variables. The powers of $\rho$ in (3.12) are chosen so that the symbol $\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}$ and the integral kernel
$$u^{(j)}(t,x,y):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{T'_yM}e^{i\varphi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)}\,\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}(t;y,\eta)\,\chi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)\,w^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)\,d\eta \tag{3.13}$$
of the operator (3.10) are scalar functions in all variables. The symbol is a genuine scalar function on $\mathbb{R}\times T'M$. Taking the square and then the fourth root in (3.12) makes the weight invariant under inversion of a single coordinate $x^\alpha$ or $y^\alpha$. On an orientable and oriented manifold, (3.12) simplifies to
$$w^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta)=[\rho(x)\,\rho(y)]^{-\frac12}\left[\det\varphi^{(j)}_{x^\alpha\eta_\beta}\right]^{\frac12}.$$

**Remark 3.5.** The existence of a phase function satisfying (i)–(iv) is nontrivial. The space of such phase functions is nonempty and path-connected, see [38, Lemmata 1.4 and 1.7].

**Remark 3.6.** A phase function satisfying (i)–(iv) from Theorem 3.3 automatically satisfies
$$\varphi^{(j)}_t(t,x^{(j)};y,\eta)+h^{(j)}(x^{(j)},\xi^{(j)})=0, \tag{3.14}$$
see, e.g., [45, Subsection 2.4.1]. The equation
$$\varphi^{(j)}_t(t,x;y,\eta)+h^{(j)}(x,\nabla\varphi^{(j)}(t,x;y,\eta))=0 \tag{3.15}$$
is known as the *eikonal equation*. Setting $x=x^{(j)}(t;y,\eta)$ in (3.15) gives (3.14). In the classical approach, (3.15) is required to hold in an open neighbourhood of
$$\{(t,x;y,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}\times M\times T'M\ |\ x=x^{(j)}(t;y,\eta)\}. \tag{3.16}$$
In the approach of the present paper, (3.15) is only required along the Hamiltonian flow, i.e. only (3.14) is needed. There is no open neighbourhood of (3.16) on which the Levi-Civita phase functions of Section 5 satisfy (3.15). Relaxing the requirement is needed to accommodate an imaginary part and so to circumvent obstructions arising from caustics.

**Remark 3.7.** If one gives up globality in time, Theorem 3.3 and its proof adapt directly to real-valued phase functions. One prescribes the phase functions to take values in $\mathbb{R}$, drops condition (iv), and replaces $\mathbb{R}$ by $(T_-,T_+)$ throughout, where
$$T_+:=\min_j\inf\Big\{t>0\,\Big|\,\det\varphi^{(j)}_{x^\alpha\eta_\beta}\big|_{x=x^{(j)}}=0,\ (y,\eta)\in T'M\Big\}, \tag{3.27}$$
$$T_-:=\max_j\sup\Big\{t<0\,\Big|\,\det\varphi^{(j)}_{x^\alpha\eta_\beta}\big|_{x=x^{(j)}}=0,\ (y,\eta)\in T'M\Big\}. \tag{3.28}$$
The values of $T_\pm$ depend on the choice of real-valued phase functions, but always $T_-<0<T_+$. Theorem 3.2 covers both the real-valued and complex-valued cases.

**Definition 3.8.** We call $\mathfrak{a}^{(j)}_0$ the *principal symbol* of the Fourier integral operator (3.10).

**Remark 3.9.** Let $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$, $x\mapsto\widetilde x$, be a (locally) invertible map. The operators $\widetilde L_\alpha:=[(\nabla_x f)^{-1}]_\alpha{}^\beta\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^\beta}$ are the pushforwards of $\partial/\partial\widetilde x^\alpha$ along $f^{-1}$. Since the $\partial/\partial\widetilde x^\alpha$ commute, so do the $\widetilde L_\alpha$. The same argument, with $f=\varphi^{(j)}_\eta$ (and accounting for the complex-valuedness of $\varphi^{(j)}$), gives an alternative explanation for the commutation of the operators $L^{(j)}_\alpha$.

**Remark 3.10.** For the massless Dirac operator, the first few equations in the hierarchy (3.32) are given by (5.9)–(5.13).

**Remark 3.11.** All statements in this section carry over verbatim to the case where $A$ is an elliptic symmetric first-order $m\times m$ matrix *pseudo*differential operator, with two exceptions:
- formulae (3.1) and (3.2) are dropped, as they are no longer true;
- "Step 2." in Subsection 3.3 is modified to account for the action of a pseudodifferential operator on an oscillatory integral invariantly, along the lines of [11, Section 4.3].

**Remark 3.12.** This section did not use the Riemannian structure of $M$. If the Riemannian density (1.1) is replaced by an arbitrary positive density, all statements and results remain the same.

## 4 Full symbols of pseudodifferential operators

**Definition 4.1.** We call *time-independent Levi-Civita phase function* the function $\phi\in C^{\infty}(M\times T'M;\mathbb{C})$ defined by

$$\phi(x;y,\eta):=\int_{\gamma}\zeta\,dz+\frac{i\epsilon}{2}\,h(y,\eta)\,[\mathrm{dist}(x,y)]^{2} \tag{4.1}$$

when $x$ lies in a geodesic neighbourhood of $y$, and continued smoothly elsewhere so that $\operatorname{Im}\phi\geq 0$. Here $\gamma$ is the unique shortest geodesic from $y$ to $x$, $\zeta$ is the parallel transport of $\eta$ along $\gamma$,

$$h(y,\eta):=\sqrt{g^{\alpha\beta}(y)\,\eta_\alpha\eta_\beta}, \tag{4.2}$$

$\mathrm{dist}$ is the geodesic distance, and $\epsilon>0$ is a parameter.

**Remark 4.2.** Formula (4.3) is effectively a special case of (3.10) with $t=0$.

**Definition 4.3.** We call *full symbol* of the operator $P$ the scalar function
$$\mathfrak{p}(y,\eta)\sim\sum_{k=-p}^{+\infty}\mathfrak{p}_{-k}(y,\eta).$$
The homogeneous functions $\mathfrak{p}_{p}$ and $\mathfrak{p}_{p-1}$ are called the *$g$-principal* and *$g$-subprincipal symbol*, respectively. (Here $g$ refers to the Riemannian metric used in the construction of the phase function $\phi$.)

**Theorem 4.4.** The invariant quantities $P_{\mathrm{sub}}$ and $\mathfrak{p}_{p-1}$ are related by

$$\mathfrak{p}_{p-1}=P_{\mathrm{sub}}+\frac{i}{2}(P_{\mathrm{prin}})_{y^\alpha\eta_\alpha}+\frac{i}{2}\,\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma}\left[\eta_\alpha(P_{\mathrm{prin}})_{\eta_\beta}\right]_{\eta_\gamma}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,g_{\beta\gamma}\left[h\,(P_{\mathrm{prin}})_{\eta_\beta}\right]_{\eta_\gamma}. \tag{4.6}$$

**Remark 4.5.** A lengthy but straightforward calculation shows that the right-hand side of (4.6) is a scalar function on the cotangent bundle. The second and third summands admit an invariant representation in terms of the Laplace–Beltrami operator of the neutral metric $n$ on $T^*M$. In local coordinates $(x^1,\dots,x^d,\xi_1,\dots,\xi_d)$ this metric is

$$n_{jk}(x,\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}-2\,\xi_\gamma\,\Gamma^{\gamma}{}_{\alpha\beta}(x)&\delta_\alpha{}^{\mu}\\\delta^{\nu}{}_{\beta}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad j,k\in\{1,\dots,2d\}. \tag{4.7}$$

"Neutral" means that $n$ has signature $(d,d)$. The neutral metric is used in developing an invariant theory of pseudodifferential operators on Riemannian manifolds. This is addressed elsewhere, see also [46].

## 5 Levi-Civita phase functions and the Dirac propagator

**Definition 5.1.** We call *positive* ($+$), resp. *negative* ($-$), *Levi-Civita phase function* the infinitely smooth function $\varphi^{\pm}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times M\times T'M;\mathbb{C})$ defined by

$$\varphi^{\pm}(t,x;y,\eta)=\int_{\gamma^{\pm}}\zeta^{\pm}\,dz+\frac{i\,\epsilon}{2}\,h(y,\eta)\,\operatorname{dist}^{2}\big(x,x^{\pm}(t;y,\eta)\big) \tag{5.4}$$

for $x$ in a geodesic neighbourhood of $x^\pm(t;y,\eta)$, and continued smoothly elsewhere so that $\operatorname{Im}\varphi^\pm\geq 0$. Here $\operatorname{dist}$ is the Riemannian geodesic distance, $\gamma^\pm$ is the shortest geodesic from $x^\pm$ to $x$, $\zeta^\pm$ is the parallel transport of $\xi^\pm(t;y,\eta)$ along $\gamma^\pm$, and $\epsilon>0$ is a parameter.

**Remark 5.2.** The time-independent phase function $\phi$ of Section 4 is the restriction to $t=0$ of the phase functions $\varphi^\pm$:
$$\phi(x;y,\eta)=\varphi^{+}(0,x;y,\eta)=\varphi^{-}(0,x;y,\eta). \tag{5.6}$$

**Definition 5.3.** The *full symbol of the positive* (resp. *negative*) *propagator* is the scalar matrix-function $\mathfrak{a}^+$ (resp. $\mathfrak{a}^-$), obtained through the algorithm of Section 3.3 with Levi-Civita phase functions. The *subprincipal symbol of the positive* (resp. *negative*) *propagator* is the scalar matrix-function $\mathfrak{a}^+_{-1}$ (resp. $\mathfrak{a}^-_{-1}$), obtained in the same way.

**Theorem 5.4.** Fix $y\in M$ and let $\{\widetilde e\}_{j=1}^{3}$ be a framing on $M$. Let $G\in C^{\infty}(M;SU(2))$ be a gauge transformation with $G(y)=\mathrm{Id}$, and let

$$e_j{}^\alpha:=\frac12\operatorname{tr}\big(s_j\,G^{*}\,s^k\,G\big)\,\widetilde e_k{}^\alpha. \tag{5.22}$$

Then

$$\nabla_\alpha G(y)=-\frac{i}{2}\Big[\overset{*}{K}{}_{\alpha\beta}(y)-\overset{*}{\widetilde K}{}_{\alpha\beta}(y)\Big]\,\sigma^\beta(y), \tag{5.23}$$

where $K$ (resp. $\widetilde K$) is the contorsion tensor of the Weitzenböck connection (see Appendix A) associated with the framing $\{e_j\}$ (resp. $\{\widetilde e_j\}$), the star denotes the Hodge dual applied in the first and third indices (see formula (A.7)), and $\sigma^\alpha(y)$ is defined by (1.3).

**Remark 5.5.** If $\{\widetilde e\}$ and $\{e\}$ are framings related by (5.22), and $\widetilde W$ and $W$ are the corresponding Dirac operators (see Definition 1.1), then
$$W=G^{*}\widetilde W G. \tag{5.24}$$

**Theorem 5.6.** We have

$$[U^{\pm}(0)]_{\mathrm{sub}}(y,\eta)=\pm\frac{1}{4\,(h(y,\eta))^{3}}\,\overset{*}{T}{}^{\alpha\beta}(y)\,\eta_\alpha\eta_\beta\,\mathrm{Id}, \tag{5.25}$$

where $T$ is the torsion tensor of the Weitzenböck connection (see Appendix A) associated with the framing $\{e_j\}$ encoded in the Dirac operator $W$ (see Definition 1.1), and the star denotes the Hodge dual applied in the second and third indices (see formula (A.6)).

## 6 Principal symbols of the Dirac propagators

**Theorem 6.1.** The principal symbols of the positive and negative Dirac propagators are

$$\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}_0(t;y,\eta)=\zeta^{\pm}(t;y,\eta)\,[v^{\pm}(y,\eta)]^{*}, \tag{6.1}$$

where $\zeta^\pm(t;y,\eta)$ is the parallel transport of $v^\pm(y,\eta)$ along $x^\pm$ with respect to the spin connection, i.e.

$$\left(\frac{d}{dt}+[\dot x^\pm]^\alpha\,\tfrac14\,\sigma_\beta\Big(\frac{\partial\sigma^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha}+\Gamma^\beta{}_{\alpha\gamma}\sigma^\gamma\Big)\right)\zeta^\pm=0,\qquad \zeta^\pm|_{t=0}=v^\pm.$$
